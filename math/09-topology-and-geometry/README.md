# Topology and Geometry

## Why This Section Matters

Topology studies the properties of spaces that are preserved under continuous deformation — it's the mathematics of shape, connectivity, and holes. Differential geometry extends this to curved spaces with notions of distance, curvature, and parallel transport. In physics, general relativity is differential geometry; in data science, topological data analysis (TDA) reveals the shape of high-dimensional datasets; in ML, manifold learning assumes data lives on low-dimensional manifolds in high-dimensional space. Riemannian geometry provides the foundation for optimization on manifolds and geometric deep learning.

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:
- Work directly from the axioms of a topology — open sets, bases, continuity, homeomorphism, separation axioms — including exhaustive computation on finite spaces.
- Prove the core point-set theorems on compactness and connectedness, and use them as invariants to show two spaces are not homeomorphic.
- Compute Gaussian curvature extrinsically and from angle excess, and explain the Theorema Egregium and Gauss–Bonnet, including the discrete angle-defect version on triangle meshes.
- Compute persistent homology of point clouds and time-delay embeddings, read Betti numbers off barcodes, and judge when a "topological feature" is real.
- Compute geodesics on a curved surface by integrating the geodesic equation, and verify them against a conserved quantity.

## Suggested Order

**Core path (🔑):** Morris, *Topology Without Tears* → Needham, *Visual Differential Geometry and Forms* → Chazal & Michel, *An Introduction to Topological Data Analysis*

The three core items are the engineer's route through this section: a free beginner's text for point-set topology, a picture-first book for curvature and geodesics, and a free tutorial survey for persistent homology. Everything else is optional depth.

1. **Tokieda — Topology & Geometry lectures** — Start with intuitive visual understanding.
2. **Morris — Topology Without Tears** — The core text for point-set topology (Ch. 1–5, then Ch. 7 on compactness): open sets, bases, continuity, homeomorphisms, connectedness and compactness. Practice 1–2.
3. **Munkres — Topology** — Optional rigor: Part I (Ch. 2–4), including the separation axioms in Ch. 4.
4. **Needham — Visual Differential Geometry** — The heart of this section: curvature, the Theorema Egregium, Gauss–Bonnet, parallel transport and geodesics. Practice 3, 5 and the mini-project.
5. **do Carmo — Riemannian Geometry** — The geodesic equation and Christoffel symbols in coordinates, for Practice 5.
6. **Chazal & Michel — An Introduction to Topological Data Analysis** — The core reading for persistent homology: complexes, barcodes and diagrams, stability, confidence sets, and GUDHI code. Practice 4.
7. **Edelsbrunner & Harer**, **Carlsson — "Topology and Data"**, and **Albin's algebraic-topology lectures** — Depth on persistence and on the homology behind it.
8. **Lee — Smooth Manifolds** — Optional graduate rigor on manifolds when ready.

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **Finite topologies by brute force** — Enumerate every topology on a 3-point set by checking the axioms over all families of subsets: you should find 29, falling into 9 homeomorphism classes. Repeat for 4 points (355 topologies, 33 classes). For each 3-point class, decide whether it is T₀, T₁, Hausdorff, and connected; then prove that every finite T₁ space is discrete. *(~3 h · Morris, Topology Without Tears Ch. 1–2 — topological spaces and bases; Munkres Ch. 2 and Ch. 4 for the separation axioms)*
2. **Point-set proof set** — Prove: (a) a continuous image of a compact space is compact; (b) a continuous image of a connected space is connected; (c) [0, 1] is compact (Heine–Borel via the "sup of the good points" argument); (d) a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism — and show by counterexamples that both hypotheses are needed; (e) ℝ is not homeomorphic to ℝ², and [0, 1) is not homeomorphic to the circle S¹ (remove a point). Optional Lean 4: prove (a) from Mathlib's open-cover characterization of compactness, then compare with the library's own proof (find it with `exact?`). *(~6 h · Morris Ch. 3–5 and 7 — connectedness, homeomorphisms, continuous mappings, compactness; Munkres Ch. 2–3; Tokieda's lectures for intuition)*
3. **Curvature you can touch** — (a) For a graph surface z = f(x, y), derive K = (f_xx·f_yy − f_xy²)/(1 + f_x² + f_y²)², and check K = 1/R² on a spherical cap and K < 0 for z = x² − y². (b) On a melon or gourd, lay narrow strips of masking tape (a strip that lies flat without wrinkling follows a geodesic) to form geodesic triangles of several sizes; measure their angle sums with a protractor and estimate K from excess ÷ area; compare with 1/(R₁R₂) from principal radii you measure with calipers or string. (c) Use the Theorema Egregium to explain why a cylinder has K = 0 and why folding a pizza slice along its length keeps the tip from drooping. *(~4 h · Needham, Visual Differential Geometry and Forms — curvature, angular excess, and the Theorema Egregium; Tokieda's lectures)*
4. **Persistent homology of signals** — With `ripser` (or GUDHI), compute barcodes for: a noisy circle (n = 300); a flat torus sampled in ℝ⁴ as (cos a, sin a, cos b, sin b); and sliding-window (time-delay) embeddings of (i) a sine, (ii) a two-tone signal with incommensurate frequencies, and (iii) white noise. Read β₀, β₁ (and β₂ for the torus) off the long bars. Then add increasing noise and show that the bottleneck distance between diagrams stays within the bound the stability theorem gives. Finish with a paragraph on exactly what a long bar does and doesn't tell you about a signal. *(~5 h · Chazal & Michel — persistence diagrams, the stability theorem, confidence regions, and the GUDHI tutorial; Edelsbrunner & Harer Ch. III, VII–VIII — complexes, persistence, and stability; Carlsson's survey "Topology and Data"; Albin's algebraic-topology lectures for homology)*
5. **Geodesics by ODE** — For a torus of revolution (tube radius 1, center-circle radius 2), derive the metric and Christoffel symbols, integrate the geodesic equations with `solve_ivp`, and verify Clairaut's relation (ρ·cos ψ is constant along a geodesic, where ρ is the distance from the axis and ψ the angle with the parallel) to 10⁻⁸. Find geodesics that oscillate around the outer equator and ones that wind over the top, and explain the difference with Clairaut. Sanity-check the same code on a sphere, where every geodesic must be a great circle. *(~4 h · Needham — geodesics and parallel transport, for the geometry; do Carmo, Riemannian Geometry — the geodesic equation and Christoffel symbols)*

**🏗️ Mini-project — Discrete curvature and Gauss–Bonnet on real meshes:** Write a small library that, for a closed triangle mesh (STL/OBJ — an icosphere, a torus, and a 3D-scanned or CAD part such as an enclosure), computes V − E + F and the genus, the angle defect (discrete Gaussian curvature) at every vertex, and the total. Verify that the defects sum to 2πχ to ~10⁻⁹ on every closed mesh — exactly 4π for any sphere-like mesh and 0 for a torus — show that defect ÷ vertex area converges to 1/R² on refined spheres (report the observed rate), and render the curvature field on the scanned part. Optional: mean curvature via the cotangent Laplacian. Draws on Needham's four proofs of the global Gauss–Bonnet theorem. Deliverable: `projects/math/09-discrete-curvature/` with code, a validation table, and renders. Success: discrete Gauss–Bonnet holds to rounding on every closed mesh, convergence is demonstrated with a measured rate, and a paragraph explains why the total is a topological invariant while the pointwise values are not. *(~10 h)*
