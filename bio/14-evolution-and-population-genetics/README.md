# Evolution and Population Genetics

## Why This Section Matters

Almost every inference in this curriculum is secretly an evolutionary argument. A BLOSUM matrix is a table of observed substitution rates. A conservation track says "selection has not let this change". gnomAD's constraint scores are a selection measurement dressed as a database column. A protein language model works because homologous sequences are correlated by shared ancestry, and a GWAS hit is almost never the causal variant — it is a marker in linkage disequilibrium with one, which is a statement about recombination history. Alignment, phylogenetics, variant interpretation and protein design all rest on population genetics, and none of the other sections teaches it.

It is also the most mathematically satisfying part of biology, and the part that behaves most like a physical theory: a handful of parameters (population size, mutation rate, recombination rate, selection coefficient), a stochastic process, and surprisingly sharp predictions about data. Much of the machinery — Markov chains, diffusion, branching processes — is already in the `math` curriculum. Two numbers decide nearly everything here: 4*Ns*, which says whether selection or drift wins, and 4*Nc*, which says how far linkage extends.

**Recommended position:** take this section after [Section 02 — Genetics and Genomics](../02-genetics-and-genomics/README.md), before or alongside [Section 04](../04-bioinformatics-and-sequence-analysis/README.md). It is numbered 14 only so that existing sections keep their numbers.

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:

- Compute the standard coalescent expectations for a sample of *n* sequences — interval lengths, time to the most recent common ancestor, total tree length, expected number of segregating sites — and check them against a simulation.
- Use 4*Ns* to decide whether a variant's fate is set by selection or by drift, and compute a new mutation's fixation probability from Kimura's formula rather than from intuition.
- Explain linkage disequilibrium as a consequence of recombination and population history, and predict the distance scale over which *r*² decays for a given effective population size.
- Correct an observed sequence divergence for multiple hits, convert it to a date with a calibrated rate, and state every assumption that date depends on.
- Read a conservation or constraint score — phyloP, phastCons, a gnomAD observed/expected ratio — as the evolutionary measurement it is, and say what it can and cannot tell you about a specific variant.

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **Derive the coalescent, then simulate it** — By hand, for a sample of *n* sequences under the standard neutral coalescent with time in units of 2*N* generations: show that the waiting time while *k* lineages remain is exponential with rate C(*k*,2), then compute E[T_MRCA] = 2(1 − 1/*n*) and the expected total tree length 2·Σ(1/*i*) for *i* = 1…*n*−1. For *n* = 20, check: Σ1/*i* = 3.5477, E[T_MRCA] = 1.90 and total tree length = 7.0955, both in units of 2*N*. With *N* = 10,000 that makes the expected T_MRCA 38,000 generations, or about 1.1 million years at 29 years per generation. Then put mutations on the tree: for a 10 kb locus with µ = 1.25 × 10⁻⁸ per base per generation, θ = 4*Nµ*L = 5, so the expected number of segregating sites in 20 samples is θ·Σ1/*i* = 17.7 and the expected pairwise difference π is 5.0 (5 × 10⁻⁴ per base). Now reproduce all five numbers in msprime over 1,000 replicates, and plot the distribution of T_MRCA against its mean — the point of the exercise is the **variance**: write 150 words on why a single locus is a terrible estimator of *N* and why that is a statement about genealogies rather than about sampling error. *(~5 h · Coop's notes, the coalescent chapters; Rosenberg & Nordborg 2002; msprime)*

2. **Linkage disequilibrium, decay and the fine-mapping problem** — Use Sved's approximation E[*r*²] ≈ 1/(1 + 4*Nc*) with *N* = 10,000 and a recombination rate of 1 cM/Mb (*c* ≈ 10⁻⁸ per base per generation) to predict *r*² at 1 kb, 10 kb, 100 kb and 1 Mb. Check: 4*Nc* = 0.4, 4, 40, 400, so E[*r*²] = 0.71, 0.20, 0.024, 0.0025. Then measure it: simulate a 1 Mb region in msprime with those parameters, compute pairwise *r*² between common variants binned by distance, and overlay the theoretical curve. Next, do the same measurement on real data — pull a 1 Mb window of 1000 Genomes genotypes for two populations with different histories and plot their LD-decay curves on the same axes. Finally, connect it to Section 02's Practice 4: given your measured decay curve, how many variants sit in credible LD with a lead SNP, and what does that imply about the number of candidate genes a single GWAS hit can implicate? Deliverable: notebook with theory, simulation and real-data curves on one plot, plus the fine-mapping implication in 200 words. *(~5 h · Coop's notes on LD and recombination; Nielsen & Slatkin, linkage disequilibrium and gene mapping; 1000 Genomes (Section 02); msprime)*

3. **When selection beats drift** — Compute a new mutation's fixation probability with Kimura's diffusion result, P_fix = (1 − e^(−4*Nsp*))/(1 − e^(−4*Ns*)) at *p* = 1/2*N*, for *N* = 10,000 and *s* = 0, 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁴, 10⁻³, 10⁻², 10⁻¹. Check: 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ (= 1/2*N*, the neutral value), 6.1 × 10⁻⁵, 2.0 × 10⁻⁴, 2.0 × 10⁻³, 1.98 × 10⁻², 0.18 — and note where Haldane's 2*s* approximation becomes good and where it fails. Plot P_fix/(1/2*N*) against 4*Ns* on log axes and mark the 4*Ns* ≈ 1 crossover. Then verify one row by Monte Carlo: run Wright-Fisher forward simulations (extend Section 02's Practice 2) for *s* = 10⁻⁴ with enough replicates to resolve a 2 × 10⁻⁴ probability — work out how many replicates that needs before you run it, which is itself the lesson. Finally, apply it: a human variant with *s* = 10⁻⁴ in a population of effective size 10,000 versus one in a population of effective size 10⁶ — same variant, different fate. Explain why, in one paragraph, and name one real human example where population size history is thought to matter. *(~4 h · Coop's notes on selection; Nielsen & Slatkin, selection in finite populations; Kimura 1968)*

4. **Multiple hits, molecular clocks and the limits of a date** — Take a set of homologous coding sequences for one gene across 8–12 species (Ensembl Compara or OrthoDB; the EMBL-EBI training in Section 04 covers retrieval). (a) Compute pairwise observed proportional differences *p*, then apply the Jukes-Cantor correction *d* = −(3/4)ln(1 − 4*p*/3). Check the correction's size: *p* = 0.2 → *d* = 0.2326 (1.16×), *p* = 0.3 → 0.3831 (1.28×), *p* = 0.5 → 0.824 (1.65×), and *p* = 0.6 → 1.207 (2.0×) — then explain what happens as *p* → 0.75 and why a saturated alignment carries no usable date. (b) Build trees two ways: a distance tree from your corrected distances, and a maximum-likelihood tree in IQ-TREE with ModelFinder choosing the substitution model and 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. Compare topologies and report which nodes are poorly supported. (c) Calibrate: using one divergence date from the literature, convert a branch length into a rate and then date a second node; then redo it with a rate 30% different and report how the date moves. Deliverable: the distance table, both trees, and a paragraph listing every assumption your date rests on — rate constancy, the calibration, the substitution model, alignment quality, and saturation. *(~6 h · Durbin et al.'s phylogenetics chapters (Section 04); IQ-TREE; Kimura 1968)*

5. **Constraint as a measurement, applied to one real variant** — Take three genes with very different evolutionary regimes — a dosage-sensitive developmental gene, a mid-constraint enzyme, and an olfactory receptor or other gene tolerant of loss of function. For each, pull from gnomAD the observed/expected ratios with their confidence intervals for synonymous, missense and loss-of-function variation, and from the UCSC Browser (Section 04's Practice 6) the phyloP and phastCons profiles across the coding exons. Then: (a) explain what an o/e of 0.1 with a wide confidence interval means for a small gene, versus 0.1 with a narrow one; (b) show that the synonymous o/e acts as the internal control, and say what a synonymous o/e far from 1 would tell you about the data rather than the gene; (c) pick one specific missense variant in the constrained gene and write the evolutionary half of an ACMG-style argument for it (see Section 08) — citing the gene-level constraint, the site-level conservation, and the population frequency — and then write the counter-argument, naming three ways this evidence can be wrong: recent selection the alignment cannot see, a human-specific function, and lineage-specific rate variation. Deliverable: the three-gene table, the conservation plots, and the two-sided argument in 400 words. *(~4 h · Karczewski et al. 2020 (Section 02); Nielsen & Slatkin on tests of neutrality; UCSC Genome Browser (Section 04))*

**🏗️ Mini-project — Is this locus under selection? A falsifiable answer:** Pick one human locus with a contested or interesting selective history — *LCT*/*MCM6*, *EDAR*, *SLC24A5*, *HBB*, *TLR* genes, or an archaic-introgressed haplotype — and build the case properly, as a genealogical argument rather than a frequency anecdote. Compute, from 1000 Genomes data in a window around the locus: the site-frequency spectrum and Tajima's *D*; a haplotype-based statistic (extended haplotype homozygosity or a derived variant of it); population differentiation (F_ST) against the genome-wide background; and LD decay around the candidate variant compared with your Practice 2 curve. Then the part that makes it science rather than storytelling: build a **neutral null** for this locus in msprime using a published demographic model for the populations you chose, run enough replicates to get an empirical distribution of each statistic, and report your observed values as percentiles of that null — not as "unusual-looking". State the inferred selection coefficient and timing if your statistics support one, with an interval. Finally, write the alternative explanation you cannot exclude (background selection, a demographic event, ascertainment or reference bias, or a mapping artefact in a repetitive region) and the single observation — ancient DNA, a functional assay, a larger sample from a specific population — that would discriminate between it and selection. Deliverable: notebook plus a 2–3 page memo in `projects/bio/14-selection-scan/`. Success criterion: every statistic is reported against a simulated neutral distribution rather than against intuition; the demographic model used for the null is named and cited; each claim is labelled measurement or inference; and the discriminating observation is specific enough that someone could go and make it. This extends Section 02's variant-history mini-project — reuse that work and cite it rather than repeating it. *(~14 h)*

1. In the standard coalescent, why is the waiting time while *k* lineages remain exponential with rate C(*k*,2), and why does most of a genealogy's total length sit in its deepest interval?

3. State the condition under which drift dominates selection. Roughly what selection coefficient becomes effectively neutral in a population of effective size 10,000?
4. Roughly what is the fixation probability of a new mutation with *s* = 0.01 in a population of 10,000? Estimate from Haldane's approximation, then say how far off it is.
5. Two populations show the same allele frequency at a site but very different haplotype structures around it. What does that tell you, and which statistic would you compute?
6. A colleague says "this variant is at 40% frequency in one population and 2% in another, so it has been under selection." Give two neutral explanations that produce the same pattern.
7. Why does an observed sequence divergence of 60% correspond to a corrected distance of about 1.2 substitutions per site, and what happens to your date estimate as the observed divergence approaches 75%?
8. Explain to a smart friend in three sentences how you can tell that a stretch of DNA matters without knowing anything about what it does.
