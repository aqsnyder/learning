# Biochemistry

## Why This Section Matters

Biochemistry bridges the gap between the abstract information flow of molecular biology and the physical, chemical reality of living systems. Proteins are not just gene products — they are molecular machines whose function depends on their three-dimensional structure, which in turn depends on amino acid chemistry. Understanding protein structure levels (primary through quaternary), enzyme kinetics, metabolic pathways, and biological thermodynamics is essential for drug design, protein engineering, and interpreting the outputs of structure prediction tools like AlphaFold. This section provides the chemical intuition needed to understand why proteins fold, how enzymes catalyze reactions, and how cells harness energy.

## Prerequisites

- [Section 00 — Molecular Biology Fundamentals](../00-molecular-biology-fundamentals/README.md)

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:

- Derive the Michaelis-Menten equation from the steady-state assumption, state exactly where that assumption fails, and explain what *k*cat/*K*m means physically and why it has a diffusion-limited ceiling.
- Compute ΔG for a reaction at real cellular concentrations rather than standard state, and use it to decide whether a coupled reaction can run.
- Explain protein folding as a balance of the hydrophobic effect, hydrogen bonding and conformational entropy — and explain why the hydrophobic effect is entropic rather than energetic.
- Predict the protonation state of any ionizable side chain at a given pH, and explain why histidine is the catalytic residue of choice near pH 7.
- Resolve Levinthal's paradox numerically: compute the absurd search time, then explain what the folding-funnel picture replaces it with.

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **Derive Michaelis-Menten twice** — Derive *v* = *V*max[S]/(*K*m+[S]) first under the rapid-equilibrium assumption, then under the steady-state assumption, and show what *K*m equals in each case. State the three conditions the steady-state derivation requires and give a concrete enzyme/substrate situation that violates each. Then: show algebraically that *k*cat/*K*m is the apparent second-order rate constant at low [S], and look up the diffusion-limited ceiling (~10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹s⁻¹) and name two enzymes that approach it. *(~3 h · Lehninger; MIT 5.07SC; Johnson & Goody 2011 for the original 1913 paper; Milo & Phillips's enzyme-turnover vignette; Voet & Voet for the rigorous treatment)*

2. **MIT 5.07SC problem sets, solutions sealed** — Work two complete problem sets from the Scholar course — one on enzyme kinetics and inhibition, one on bioenergetics — without looking at the posted solutions. Time yourself. Then grade against the solutions and keep a one-line error log for each miss, classified as "concept", "algebra", or "units". Deliverable: the error log. If more than a third are "concept", re-read the corresponding Lehninger chapter before moving on. *(~5 h · MIT 5.07SC problem sets and solutions)*

3. **ATP's real ΔG, and what it can pay for** — ΔG°′ for ATP hydrolysis is about −30.5 kJ/mol. Using cellular concentrations ([ATP] ≈ 3 mM, [ADP] ≈ 0.5 mM, [Pi] ≈ 5 mM, pH 7, 37 °C), compute the actual ΔG. Then: (a) how many orders of magnitude from equilibrium is the cell holding this reaction? (b) a biosynthetic step has ΔG°′ = +25 kJ/mol — how many ATP equivalents must be coupled to make it proceed at 10:1 product:reactant? (c) the proton-motive force is ~180 mV — how many protons per ATP does that imply the ATP synthase needs, and does your answer match the measured stoichiometry? Check your actual ΔG against the range Milo & Phillips report for real cells. *(~3 h · Lehninger thermodynamics chapters; MIT 7.05; Milo & Phillips, "How Much Energy Is Released in ATP Hydrolysis?"; HHMI BioInteractive *ATP Synthesis* for (c))*

4. **Titration curves and the histidine argument** — Plot (in Python) the titration curve and net-charge-versus-pH curve for free histidine, glutamate and lysine from their p*K*a values. Then compute the net charge of a short peptide of your choice at pH 5.0, 7.4 and 9.0. Finally, compute buffer capacity dβ/dpH for a histidine-imidazole buffer and show it peaks at the p*K*a — and write two sentences on why that makes His both the best physiological buffer side chain and the best general acid-base catalyst. *(~2.5 h · Lehninger; AK Lectures for the kinetics/pH review)*

5. **Mechanism write-up from the structure itself** — Pick one enzyme covered by PDB-101's Molecule of the Month (serine proteases, carbonic anhydrase and lysozyme are all good choices). Open the actual PDB entry, find the catalytic residues *by their real residue numbers in a specific chain*, measure the key distances in a viewer (ChimeraX or PyMOL), and then write 300 words describing the mechanism with every residue named by number. Deliverable: the write-up plus a figure with the active site labelled. Check: your residue numbers must match the primary literature's, and if they don't, work out whether it's a numbering-convention difference or your error. *(~3 h · PDB-101 and UCSF ChimeraX, both listed in [Section 03](../03-structural-biology/README.md); Lehninger's protein-structure chapters for structural vocabulary)*

6. **Levinthal, then the funnel** — Compute the Levinthal estimate: a 100-residue chain with 3 accessible conformations per residue, sampled at 10⁻¹³ s per conformation — how long to search exhaustively, in units of the age of the universe? Then read Zwanzig, Szabo & Bagchi and Dill & MacCallum and write 400 words explaining what is wrong with the premise, not the arithmetic: how do funnel topography, local structure formation and hydrophobic collapse reduce the search? Before the write-up, size the driving force: from Milo & Phillips's hydrophobic-effect vignette, estimate the free energy of burying the nonpolar side chains of your 100-residue protein, and explain in two sentences why at room temperature that term is mostly entropic — what the water is doing. End with the one thing Dill and MacCallum say is still unsolved. *(~3 h · Zwanzig, Szabo & Bagchi 1992; Dill & MacCallum, "The Protein-Folding Problem, 50 Years On"; Milo & Phillips's hydrophobic-effect vignette; Lehninger on the hydrophobic effect)*

**🏗️ Mini-project — Enzyme assay, simulated end to end:** Build the full measurement chain for a spectrophotometric enzyme assay in one notebook, then use it to find out what your instrument actually limits. Simulate absorbance-versus-time traces for an enzyme with known *k*cat and *K*m at eight substrate concentrations, with realistic non-idealities: detector read noise, 1/f drift in the lamp, a small baseline offset, finite sampling rate, and substrate depletion over the measurement window. Then run the analysis you would actually run: extract initial rates by linear fit over a chosen window, fit Michaelis-Menten by nonlinear least squares, and bootstrap the residuals to get confidence intervals on *k*cat and *K*m. Finally, sweep the non-idealities one at a time and report which one corrupts *K*m most and which corrupts *V*max most. Also fit the same data by Lineweaver-Burk and show numerically why the double-reciprocal transform biases the estimate. Deliverable: a notebook in `projects/bio/01-enzyme-assay/` plus a one-page memo written as an instrument spec — the minimum detector noise, sampling rate and measurement window needed to get *K*m to ±10%. Success criterion: your bootstrap confidence intervals cover the true values at the nominal rate, and your memo correctly identifies baseline drift versus read noise as dominating different parameters. *(~10 h)*
