# Bioinstrumentation, Imaging and Lab Automation

## Why This Section Matters

Every other section in this curriculum consumes data. This one is about where the data comes from. A sequencing read, a fluorescence image, a patch-clamp trace, a plate-reader absorbance and a flow-cytometry event are all the output of a signal chain: a transducer, an amplifier, a filter, a converter, and a long list of compromises between throughput, resolution and noise. Those compromises decide which biological questions are answerable at all — the reason single-cell transcriptomics exists is a microfluidic droplet and a cheap base, and the reason the cryo-EM revolution happened when it did was a detector, not a microscope.

This is the section written in your own language. Numerical aperture is an étendue argument; a camera's read noise and a photomultiplier's dark count are the same budget you keep for an ADC; a patch clamp is a transimpedance amplifier with a gigaohm feedback resistor and a capacitance problem; a self-driving lab is a HIL rig with pipettes. Reading biology through its instruments is the fastest way in for someone who already knows what a noise floor is — and it is also how you tell, when you read a paper, whether a surprising result is biology or an artefact of the apparatus.

**Recommended position:** take this section after [Section 03 — Structural Biology](../03-structural-biology/README.md), as the "how the data is made" counterpart to Section 04's "what to do with the data". It is numbered 13 only so that existing sections keep their numbers.

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:

- Derive the diffraction limit of a microscope objective from its numerical aperture, choose a camera pixel pitch that satisfies Nyquist at the sample, and say what super-resolution methods trade away to beat the limit.
- Build a photon budget for a fluorescence measurement — photons at the detector, quantum efficiency, read noise, dark current — and say which term dominates and what you would change first.
- Specify a patch-clamp or extracellular front end: feedback resistance against current-noise density, pipette capacitance against bandwidth, series resistance against voltage error.
- Account for a sequencing run end to end: clusters or pores, read length, cycle time, raw data volume, per-base error profile, and the coverage a given analysis therefore requires.
- Judge an assay's quality from its controls (Z′, CV, signal window) and compute the throughput of an automated screening line, including where its real bottleneck is.

## 🛠️ Practice

*Every exercise here is a calculation, a specification or an analysis of public data — there is no instrument to build and nothing to operate. If a facility ever gives you hands-on time on the real hardware, note that confocal, STED and flow-cytometry instruments are Class 3B/4 laser systems inside interlocked enclosures and electrophysiology rigs sit on mains-powered amplifiers: work to that facility's laser-safety and electrical-safety rules and its training requirements, not to this page.*

1. **Resolution, sampling and the pixel you should buy** — For λ = 500 nm, compute Abbe's limit λ/2NA and the Rayleigh criterion 0.61λ/NA at NA = 0.5, 1.0 and 1.4. Check: at NA = 1.4 you get 179 nm and 218 nm; at NA = 0.5, 500 nm and 610 nm. Then do the sampling calculation that decides a purchase: to sample at Nyquist you need a pixel spacing at the sample of about half the Rayleigh distance (≈ 109 nm at NA = 1.4), so compute the required camera pixel pitch at 40×, 60× and 100× magnification — 4.4 µm, 6.5 µm and 10.9 µm. Finally, state what happens in both failure directions: a 6.5 µm camera on a 100× objective (empty magnification, photons spread over too many pixels) and on a 20× objective (undersampling, resolution thrown away). Deliverable: a table of the nine resolution values plus the three pitch values, and two sentences on each failure mode. *(~2.5 h · MicroscopyU — Resolution and Digital Imaging; "Matching camera to microscope resolution")*

2. **Photon budget for a fluorescence measurement** — Model a camera pixel: signal electrons = photons × QE, total noise = √(shot + dark + read²). With QE = 0.7, read noise 1.5 e⁻ rms, dark current 0.01 e⁻/s and a 100 ms exposure, compute SNR at 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 photons per pixel. Check: 100 photons gives 70 e⁻ of signal against 8.5 e⁻ of noise, SNR ≈ 8.2, and read noise is only ~3% of the variance there. Then solve for the photons needed to reach SNR = 10: ≈ 146 with 1.5 e⁻ read noise, against a shot-noise-only floor of 143 — and ≈ 183 if the read noise is 6 e⁻. Conclude with the engineering question: given those numbers, when is a cooled low-read-noise camera worth buying, and when is the honest answer "collect more photons"? Then extend the model by adding photobleaching as an exponential decay in emitted photons and show what that does to the optimal exposure time. Deliverable: notebook, the SNR table, the bleaching trade-off curve, and a 200-word recommendation. *(~4 h · MicroscopyU CCD noise-sources and SNR tutorials; [physics/12](../../physics/12-measurement-noise-and-sensor-physics/README.md) for the noise physics)*

3. **Specify a patch-clamp front end** — A voltage-clamp headstage is a transimpedance amplifier, so size it like one. (a) Thermal current noise of the feedback resistor is √(4kTB/R): at 295 K compute the density and the rms over a 1 kHz and a 10 kHz bandwidth for R_f = 50 MΩ, 500 MΩ, 5 GΩ and 50 GΩ. Check: 500 MΩ gives 5.7 fA/√Hz and 0.18 pA rms in 1 kHz; 5 GΩ gives 1.8 fA/√Hz. (b) A single 20 pS channel at 50 mV driving force passes 1 pA — compute the SNR against each of those noise floors in a 1 kHz band (≈ 5.5 with 500 MΩ, ≈ 17.5 with 5 GΩ) and state the minimum R_f for a usable single-channel recording. (c) Now the bandwidth cost: with pipette series resistance R_s and cell capacitance C_m, the clamp's −3 dB bandwidth is 1/(2πR_sC_m) — tabulate it for R_s = 5, 10, 20 MΩ and C_m = 5, 10 pF (R_s = 10 MΩ with C_m = 10 pF gives 1.6 kHz, which will visibly distort a fast sodium current). (d) Compute the series-resistance voltage error I·R_s for I = 1, 5, 10 nA at each R_s, and say at what current the clamp has stopped clamping. Deliverable: a one-page specification with every term in A/√Hz, A rms, Hz or V, and a final paragraph on which single component choice you would change first and why. *(~4 h · The Axon Guide Ch. 3, 6 and 11; [physics/12](../../physics/12-measurement-noise-and-sensor-physics/README.md); [pcb/12](../../pcb/12-analog-mixed-signal-and-precision-measurement/README.md) for the circuit side)*

4. **Account for a sequencing run, platform against platform** — For a short-read run of 2 × 10⁹ clusters at 2 × 150 bp over 44 h, compute total output and throughput: 600 Gb per run, ≈ 13.6 Gb/h, and ≈ 6.5 human genomes at 30× (93 Gb each), so ≈ 6.8 h of instrument time per genome. Then the data-handling side: at 1 Gpx per image, 12 bits, four channels and 300 cycles, the raw imaging data is ≈ 1.8 TB per run — compute the sustained write bandwidth that implies and compare it to the basecalled output. Now the error profile: convert Q20/Q30/Q40 to error rates (1 in 100 / 1,000 / 10,000), and for a long-read platform at 95%, 99% and 99.9% per-base accuracy compute the expected errors in a 10 kb read (500, 100, 10) and the probability that such a read is error-free. Finish with the analysis consequence: for each platform pick one task from Sections 02 and 04 — SNV calling, structural-variant detection, or assembling a repeat array — and say which platform can do it and which cannot, citing your own numbers. Deliverable: a two-platform comparison table plus the task verdicts. *(~3.5 h · Goodwin et al. 2016 (Section 02); Deamer, Akeson & Branton 2016)*

5. **Validate an assay, then find the real bottleneck of the line** — (a) **Assay quality.** Zhang, Chung & Oldenburg's Z′ = 1 − 3(σ_p + σ_n)/|μ_p − μ_n| is the standard screening metric (*J. Biomol. Screen.* 4(2):67–73, 1999). Compute it for μ_p = 100, μ_n = 10 with (σ_p, σ_n) = (5, 2), (12, 8) and (20, 15) — you should get 0.77, 0.33 and −0.17 — and say which of those assays you would run a 100,000-compound screen on. Then invert it: for that same 90-unit signal window, what is the largest equal control SD that still gives Z′ ≥ 0.5? (7.5, i.e. a 7.5% CV at the high control.) (b) **Hit calling.** With a 3σ threshold, compute the per-well false-positive rate (0.135%) and the number of false hits in a 100,000-well single-point screen (≈ 135) — then state how many confirmation replicates you would run and what that costs in plates. (c) **Throughput.** For a four-step protocol of 45 s dispense, 30 min incubation, 45 s detection-reagent addition and 90 s plate read, compute serial time per plate (1,980 s → 14.5 plates per 8 h) and then the pipelined steady-state rate set by the slowest non-incubator step (90 s → 40 plates/h, 320 plates or 122,880 wells per 8 h), plus the number of incubator slots Little's law requires to sustain it (⌈1800/90⌉ = 20). Say what you would buy first: a second reader, a second dispenser, or more incubator slots. Deliverable: the Z′ table, the hit-confirmation plan, and the throughput model with its bottleneck named. *(~4 h · Assay Guidance Manual; [Section 12](../12-statistics-and-experimental-design/README.md) for the error-rate reasoning; PyLabRobot for an implementation)*

**🏗️ Mini-project — One instrument, specified from the physics up:** Pick one instrument that produces data used elsewhere in this curriculum — a confocal or spinning-disk fluorescence microscope, a short-read sequencer, a 384-well plate reader, a flow cytometer, or a high-density electrophysiology rig — and write the specification document its vendor will not give you. Four parts. **(i) Signal chain**: a block diagram from the physical quantity to the stored number, with every stage labelled and the units at each interface. **(ii) Noise and resolution budget**: every term you can estimate, in the right units, with the dominant one identified and the arithmetic shown — for an optical instrument that means photons, QE, read noise and dark current; for electrophysiology, the resistor and amplifier noise from Practice 3; for a sequencer, cycle-level error and the coverage needed to overcome it. **(iii) Throughput and data budget**: samples per hour, bytes per sample, where the pipeline's bottleneck actually sits. **(iv) Failure modes**: three specific ways this instrument produces plausible-looking wrong data (spectral bleed-through, index hopping, electrode drift, well-position effects, sorter coincidence — name real ones), and for each, the control or calibration that would catch it. Then validate what you can against a real dataset: pull one public dataset produced by that instrument class and check at least two of your budget numbers against it (measured background and noise in a real image; per-base quality distribution in real FASTQ; control-well CV in a real screening plate). Deliverable: a 3–4 page specification with figures, in `projects/bio/13-instrument-spec/`. Success criterion: every number has units and a stated source — datasheet, your own calculation, or your own measurement from the public data — the dominant noise term is identified and defended, and at least two of your predicted numbers are checked against real data with the discrepancy explained. *(~12 h)*

## ✅ Check Yourself

Answer from memory first, then check against the sources — or paste this section into NotebookLM and ask it to quiz you.

1. State the Abbe diffraction limit and the Rayleigh criterion, and roughly what each gives for green light through an NA 1.4 objective. What does a higher NA cost you?
2. Your fluorescence image is noisy. How do you tell whether you are read-noise-limited or shot-noise-limited, and what do you change in each case?
3. STED and single-molecule localization both beat the diffraction limit. Explain the mechanism of each in one sentence, and name what each pays for the resolution.
4. In a patch-clamp headstage, why does a larger feedback resistor lower the current noise? What limits how large you can make it?
5. Roughly how many 30× human genomes come off one high-output short-read run, and roughly how much raw image data does that run generate? Estimate, then check against your own calculation.
6. An assay has Z′ = 0.3. What does that number mean operationally, and what are your two options before screening 100,000 compounds?
7. A colleague says "we automated the protocol, so throughput went up 10×." What would you measure before believing that, and where is the bottleneck most likely to be?
8. Explain to a smart friend in three sentences why better detectors — not better lenses or better microscopes — set off the cryo-EM revolution.
