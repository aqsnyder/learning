# Statistics and Experimental Design for Biology

## Why This Section Matters

Every section after this one produces numbers: a *p*-value, a fold change, a docking score, a pLDDT, a cluster count. None of them means anything without the judgement this section teaches. Biology is the field where the multiple-testing problem became unavoidable — a single RNA-seq run asks 20,000 questions at once, and a 5% error rate per question means a thousand wrong answers per experiment. It is also the field where the replication crisis was first quantified, where batch effects have overturned high-profile published findings, and where the most consequential statistical decision — how many replicates, processed in what order, by whom — is made before any data exists.

For a measurement engineer this is the most transferable section in the curriculum. Biological replicates are an uncertainty budget by another name; batch effects are drift and systematic error; a power calculation is a detection-threshold calculation; `n = 3` is a sample-size choice that would never survive in a calibration lab. The difference is that biological noise is larger, less stationary, and harder to characterize than anything on a bench, which makes the discipline matter more rather than less.

**Recommended position:** take this section alongside or just before [Section 04 — Bioinformatics and Sequence Analysis](../04-bioinformatics-and-sequence-analysis/README.md), and before Sections 05, 08 and 10. It is numbered 12 only so that existing sections keep their numbers.

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:

- Explain what a *p*-value is and is not, in the ASA statement's terms, and state what a 0.05 threshold costs you when you apply it 20,000 times.
- Apply Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg correctly, say which error rate each controls (FWER versus FDR), and demonstrate by simulation that BH's realized false-discovery proportion lands near its nominal *q*.
- Compute the sample size needed for a target power at a stated effect size, and say what an underpowered experiment does to the *published* effect sizes in a field.
- Distinguish a batch effect from a biological effect, show what a confounded design does to a false-discovery rate, and design the balanced or blocked alternative.
- Explain why count data needs a negative-binomial rather than a Poisson or Gaussian model, and show what happens to the error rate if you ignore overdispersion.

## Suggested Order

**Core path (🔑):** Holmes & Huber, *Modern Statistics for Modern Biology* → Benjamini & Hochberg, false discovery rate (1995)

1. **Krzywinski & Altman — the first four *Points of Significance* columns** — One evening. "Importance of being uncertain", "Error bars", "Significance, *P* values and *t*-tests", "Power and sample size" give you the vocabulary before the textbook assumes it
2. **Wasserstein & Lazar — the ASA statement** — Two pages, and the cheapest correction to your own writing you will ever make
3. **Holmes & Huber, *Modern Statistics for Modern Biology* — the early chapters** — Generative models for discrete data and the statistical-modelling chapters: distributions that actually describe biological data, and maximum likelihood on them
4. **Benjamini & Hochberg (1995)** — Read the original. Then implement the procedure from the paper's own description in ten lines, which is all it takes

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **The three corrections, on data where you know the answer** — Simulate a differential-expression experiment: 20,000 genes, 200 of them genuinely changed by 2 standard deviations, 10 samples per group, Gaussian noise. Run a two-sample *t*-test per gene, then apply (a) no correction at α = 0.05, (b) Bonferroni, (c) Benjamini-Hochberg at *q* = 0.05. For each, report total discoveries, true positives, false positives, and the realized false-discovery proportion. Expect roughly: uncorrected ≈ 1,190 discoveries, of which ~990 are false (0.05 × 19,800 nulls) and ~198 of the 200 true genes are found; Bonferroni (threshold 2.5 × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 15 discoveries, essentially all true, so you have traded 180 real genes for certainty; BH ≈ 108 discoveries of which ~102 are true and ~6 false, realized FDR ≈ 0.05 — the nominal *q* delivered. Then plot the *p*-value histogram and estimate the null fraction π₀ from its flat part (with λ = 0.5 you should get ≈ 0.99, give or take 0.01 from run to run, which is essentially the truth. Storey's estimator is biased upward, the conservative direction, but with ~99% power almost no true-gene *p*-values exceed 0.5, so the bias is negligible here). Finally rerun the whole thing with 3 samples per group: BH now returns **nothing**, while the uncorrected analysis still "finds" about a thousand genes — write two sentences on which of those two results is the honest one. *(~4 h · Benjamini & Hochberg 1995; Holmes & Huber, the testing chapter; "Comparing samples—part II")*

2. **Sample size before data, as a design calculation** — Using `statsmodels`' power tools or your own `scipy.stats.nct` calculation, build the table that should exist before any experiment: for a two-sample *t*-test at α = 0.05 two-sided and 80% power, the *n* per group needed at Cohen's *d* = 0.5, 0.8, 1.0 and 2.0. Check: 64, 26, 17 and 6. Then invert it — at *n* = 3 per group, what is the power to detect *d* = 2.0? (≈ 0.46, so a coin flip for an effect of two full standard deviations.) Now the part that matters: simulate 100,000 underpowered experiments (*n* = 3, *d* = 0.5), keep only those reaching *p* < 0.05, and plot the distribution of their *estimated* effect sizes against the true 0.5. Expect power ≈ 0.08, a mean estimated |*d*| among the "significant" results of about 3.3 — roughly 6.6× the truth — and about 9% of those significant results pointing in the **wrong direction**. Deliverable: the power table, the winner's-curse histogram, and 150 words connecting this to why large replication studies find smaller effects than the originals. *(~4 h · "Power and sample size"; "Replication"; Ioannidis 2005)*

3. **Batch effects: one design choice worth more than any analysis** — Simulate 10,000 genes, 200 truly changed by 2 SD, 10 samples per arm, with a per-gene batch shift drawn at 1 SD, under three designs. (a) **Confounded**: every control sample processed in batch 1, every treated sample in batch 2, analysed with a plain *t*-test. (b) **Balanced**: 10 batches, each containing one control and one treated sample, analysed with a plain *t*-test that ignores batch. (c) **Balanced and blocked**: the same data as (b), analysed with a paired *t*-test (or batch as a covariate in a linear model). Report discoveries, true positives and realized FDR for each at BH *q* = 0.05. Expect approximately: (a) ~4,700 discoveries of which ~97% are false — a nominal 5% FDR delivering 97%; (b) only a handful of discoveries (≈ 7 on average, anywhere from 0 to ~15 from run to run), all true, but only ~4% power, because the unmodelled batch shift is inflating your residual variance; (c) ~58 discoveries, ~55 true, realized FDR ≈ 0.05. Then write the one-page bench protocol that follows from this: what you randomize, what you block, and the five things you record for every sample. *(~5 h · Leek et al. 2010; "Analysis of variance and blocking"; "Designing comparative experiments")*

4. **Counts are not Gaussian, and they are not Poisson either** — First the arithmetic: for a negative-binomial model with mean μ = 100 and dispersion α, the variance is μ + αμ². Tabulate variance, SD and CV for α = 0, 0.01, 0.1, 0.4 — check that α = 0.1 gives variance 1,100 (CV 0.33) against the Poisson's 100 (CV 0.10). Then show what ignoring that costs: simulate 20,000 genes with **no** true differential expression — negative-binomial counts, mean 100, dispersion α = 0.05, 5 samples per group — then throw away the replicate structure the way a naive Poisson analysis does: sum each group's counts and test the group split with a two-sided binomial test against *p* = 0.5. You should find roughly 42% of genes at *p* < 0.05 instead of 5%, and about 67% if α = 0.2. Write one sentence naming exactly which assumption that test made and what it cost. Now do it properly: take a public bulk RNA-seq count matrix (`pydeseq2`'s example data, or the airway dataset), run DESeq2 or pydeseq2, and inspect three diagnostics — the dispersion-versus-mean fit, the *p*-value histogram, and an MA plot before and after log-fold-change shrinkage. Deliverable: the variance table, the null false-positive rate, and the three diagnostic plots with one sentence each on what a pathological version would look like. *(~6 h · Love, Huber & Anders 2014; Holmes & Huber, the count-data chapters)*

5. **Re-derive Ioannidis in twenty lines** — Implement the positive-predictive-value model from Ioannidis (2005): PPV as a function of the pre-study odds *R*, α, β and the bias term *u*. Check these values: at *R* = 0.1, α = 0.05, power 0.8 and no bias, PPV = 0.615; add bias *u* = 0.3 and it falls to 0.204; at *R* = 0.01 it is 0.138 without bias and 0.025 with; at *R* = 1/3 it is 0.842. Then map four real research settings onto the parameters and defend your choice of *R* for each: a candidate-gene association study, a well-powered GWAS with a 5 × 10⁻⁸ threshold, a confirmatory phase III trial, and an exploratory single-cell screen. Deliverable: a small function, the four-row table, and 300 words on which parameter is easiest to improve in practice — and why "more power" is usually the wrong answer. *(~3 h · Ioannidis 2005; Wasserstein & Lazar 2016)*

**🏗️ Mini-project — Pre-register, then re-analyse:** Take one published dataset with public counts or measurements and an accessible paper — GEO, Zenodo or `recount3` all work — and do the two halves in order, without peeking. **Half 1, before you look at the data:** write a pre-analysis plan stating the question, the model, the normalization, the exact test, the multiple-testing procedure and threshold, the covariates you will include (with the batch and design variables you extracted from the metadata), the power you have at a stated effect size, and the criteria that would make you declare the analysis uninformative. Commit it with a timestamp. **Half 2:** run the plan as written. Then, and only then, run the three sanity checks that should accompany any omics result — the *p*-value histogram, a PCA coloured by every available technical variable, and a permutation of the condition labels to confirm your pipeline returns a null result on null data. Report your discoveries against the paper's, and account for every difference as a design choice, a preprocessing difference, a threshold difference, or a disagreement. Deliverable: the pre-registration, the notebook and a 2-page report in `projects/bio/12-stats-audit/`. Success criterion: the permutation test returns approximately the expected number of discoveries (near zero at your threshold); your PCA either rules out batch structure or identifies it; and every deviation from your own pre-analysis plan is listed, with its justification, in a section titled "where I departed from the plan". *(~12 h)*

## ✅ Check Yourself

Answer from memory first, then check against the sources — or paste this section into NotebookLM and ask it to quiz you.

1. State what a *p*-value is, precisely. Then state three things it is not, in the ASA statement's terms.
2. What is the difference between the family-wise error rate and the false-discovery rate, and which one does Benjamini-Hochberg control? What is BH's assumption about dependence between tests?
3. You test 20,000 genes at α = 0.05 with no correction and none of them is truly differential. Roughly how many "hits" do you get, and what does the *p*-value histogram look like?
4. Two labs run the same comparison. Lab A processes all controls on Monday and all treated samples on Tuesday; Lab B alternates. Both report a nominal 5% FDR. Which number should you not believe, and why is the problem invisible in Lab A's own data?
5. Roughly how many samples per group do you need to detect a one-standard-deviation difference at 80% power and α = 0.05? Estimate first, then compute it.
6. Why does RNA-seq need a negative binomial rather than a Poisson? What biological quantity does the dispersion parameter stand in for, and why does DESeq2 shrink it across genes?
7. A colleague says "the effect was significant, so with more samples it would only get more significant." Give two reasons that can be false.
8. Explain to a smart friend in three sentences why an experiment that is too small is not just inconclusive but actively misleading about the size of the effect.
