# Protein Engineering and Design

## Why This Section Matters

Proteins are the molecular machines of life — enzymes that catalyze reactions, antibodies that recognize pathogens, and structural scaffolds that give cells their shape. For decades, engineering new proteins required either random mutagenesis (directed evolution) or painstaking physics-based modeling (Rosetta). The deep learning revolution has shattered these limitations: protein language models like ESM-2 learn the grammar of protein sequences from evolutionary data, RFdiffusion generates entirely novel protein structures from scratch, and ProteinMPNN designs sequences that fold into any target backbone. This section covers both the classical foundations and the AI-powered frontier of protein engineering — arguably the most transformative area in all of computational biology.

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:

- Distinguish the four problems this field solves — structure prediction, inverse folding, generative design, and sequence-space generation — and say which tool addresses which.
- Run a protein language model to produce zero-shot variant-effect scores, and calibrate them against a published deep mutational scan rather than trusting them.
- Execute the standard design-evaluation loop (backbone → ProteinMPNN sequences → structure prediction → self-consistency RMSD and pLDDT filtering) and interpret each metric's blind spots.
- Explain why directed evolution works at all, in terms of library size, mutation neutrality and the local structure of a fitness landscape.
- Compare the in-silico pass rates reported in design papers against their experimental hit rates, and state what the filters actually buy.

## Suggested Order

**Core path (🔑):** Arnold's Nobel lecture on directed evolution (2018) → Lin et al., ESM-2 / ESMFold (2023) → Watson et al., RFdiffusion (2023)

1. **Arnold Nobel Lecture (2018)** — Start with the historical foundation; understand directed evolution before learning how computation is replacing it
2. **Huang, Boyken & Baker (2016)** — Review of Rosetta-based de novo design provides the physics-based context for understanding why deep learning approaches were needed
3. **Rives et al. (2021)** — The original ESM paper shows how protein language models learn biological structure from sequence data alone
4. **Lin et al. (2023) — ESM-2** — See how scaling protein language models enables atomic-resolution structure prediction directly from sequence
5. **Watson et al. (2023) — RFdiffusion** — The breakthrough paper on generative diffusion models for protein structure design
6. **Dauparas et al. (2022) — ProteinMPNN** — Understand the inverse folding problem: given a structure, design the sequence that folds into it
7. **Wicky et al. (2022)** — "Hallucination" as a design paradigm — connects structure prediction networks to generative design
8. **Madani et al. (2023) — ProGen** — Autoregressive generation of functional proteins — a different paradigm from diffusion-based design
9. **Hayes et al. (2025) — ESM3** — Generative design from a single model over sequence, structure, and function
10. **Baker Nobel Lecture + Nobel scientific background (2024)** — Step back and see how prediction and design converged into a Nobel-winning field
11. **Rosetta Commons** — Install and explore the tools; try the introductory tutorials for protein design (free for academic and non-profit use; check the licence otherwise)
12. **ProteinGym, then the RFdiffusion and ProteinMPNN code** — Get the deep-mutational-scan data for Practice 1, and open the RFdiffusion Colab notebook and the ProteinMPNN repository before Practices 3–4
13. **Before you design anything intended for the bench: the dual-use problem** — Generative design tools are dual-use, and nucleic-acid synthesis screening is the control point between a design and a physical molecule. Read Urbina et al. (2022), listed in [Section 10](../10-ai-for-biology/README.md), and the biosecurity subsection of [Section 11](../11-frontiers/README.md), which covers how screening is being strengthened against exactly the design methods in this section. The directed-evolution theory behind these methods is in [Section 14](../14-evolution-and-population-genetics/README.md)

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **Calibrate ESM against a real DMS** — Pick a protein with a public deep mutational scan (ProteinGym aggregates many; TEM-1 β-lactamase, GFP and PTEN are all well-studied). Run ESM-2 (the 650M checkpoint is enough on free Colab) and compute masked-marginal log-ratio scores for every single-point mutant. Correlate against the measured fitness with Spearman ρ. Then build two baselines: a PSSM from a jackhmmer MSA of the same protein, and a simple hydrophobicity/burial heuristic. Report all three correlations in one table. Deliverable: notebook plus the table, plus 200 words on where ESM beats the MSA baseline and where it does not — and what that implies about what the model learned. *(~5 h · Lin et al. 2023 (ESM-2); Rives et al. 2021; ProteinGym)*

2. **The arithmetic of directed evolution** — From Arnold's Nobel lecture, work the numbers. For a 300-residue protein: how many distinct single mutants exist? Double mutants? What fraction of all doubles does a 10⁷-member library cover? Given that roughly a third of random missense mutations are substantially destabilising, what is the expected fraction of a library that is still folded after 1, 3 and 5 rounds of mutagenesis at 2 mutations per gene? Then simulate: build a toy fitness landscape with tunable epistasis, and compare greedy single-step hill climbing, recombination between two hill-climbed variants, and random search at matched library size. Show the regime where recombination wins. Deliverable: the worked numbers plus the simulation notebook. *(~4 h · Arnold Nobel lecture 2018; Huang, Boyken & Baker 2016 for the contrast with computational design)*

3. **Inverse folding self-consistency loop** — Take a native backbone from the PDB. Run ProteinMPNN at two or three sampling temperatures to generate ~50 sequences each. Measure native sequence recovery per temperature. Then fold every designed sequence with ESMFold or ColabFold and compute self-consistency RMSD to the original backbone plus mean pLDDT. Report the fraction passing scRMSD < 2 Å at each temperature, and plot recovery against pass rate to expose the trade-off. Deliverable: notebook and the trade-off plot. Then answer in writing: why is sequence recovery a bad primary metric for design quality? *(~5 h · Dauparas et al. 2022 (ProteinMPNN); ProteinMPNN code; Lin et al. 2023 (ESMFold))*

4. **Run RFdiffusion and account for every design** — Using the public Colab notebook, run RFdiffusion to scaffold a small functional motif (a short helical binding motif or a two-residue metal site is tractable). Generate ~50 backbones, pass each through ProteinMPNN, fold the sequences, and apply the standard in-silico filters (scRMSD < 2 Å, pLDDT > 80, motif RMSD to the target geometry). Record the pass rate at each stage as a funnel. Then find the experimental success rates reported in Watson et al. and write 300 words comparing your in-silico funnel to their wet-lab hit rate: what fraction of in-silico passers are expected to work experimentally, and what is the filter therefore buying — enrichment, or false confidence? *(~6 h · Watson et al. 2023 (RFdiffusion); RFdiffusion code and its Colab notebook; Dauparas et al. 2022 and the ProteinMPNN code)*

5. **Two paradigms, one page** — Write a one-page comparison of ProGen (autoregressive sequence generation) against RFdiffusion (structure-space diffusion). Cover: what each can express that the other cannot; what each needs as conditioning input; the evidence each paper offers and the strength of that evidence (how many designs, which assay, what success rate); and the failure modes you would predict for each. End with one concrete experiment that would discriminate between the two approaches on a task they both claim. *(~3 h · Madani et al. 2023 (ProGen); Watson et al. 2023 (RFdiffusion); Huang, Boyken & Baker 2016)*

6. **Hallucination in miniature** — Reproduce the core mechanism of Wicky et al. at toy scale. Starting from a random 60-residue sequence, run Metropolis Monte Carlo over point mutations with an objective that maximises predicted confidence (mean pLDDT from ESMFold, which is fast enough to iterate). Run a few hundred steps and plot the pLDDT trajectory and the predicted structure at several checkpoints. Then the critical part: write 200 words on why a high-confidence prediction is not evidence of a foldable protein, and what the published method adds to stop you from fooling yourself. *(~5 h · Wicky et al. 2022 (hallucinating symmetric assemblies); Lin et al. 2023)*

**🏗️ Mini-project — Design and triage a small binder:** Pick a published target with a small, well-defined epitope (a viral receptor-binding domain or a cytokine with known binder work gives you literature to check yourself against). Run the full modern stack: RFdiffusion to generate binder backbones against the epitope → ProteinMPNN for sequences → ColabFold or ESMFold validation of the monomer and the complex → filtering on scRMSD, pLDDT, interface PAE, predicted interface contacts and buried surface area. Generate enough designs that the funnel is meaningful (aim for 200+ backbones). Deliverable: a notebook in `projects/bio/07-binder-design/` plus a one-page design review that lists your top 5 designs with every metric value, states explicitly what you believe and do not believe about each, names the dominant failure mode among the designs that did not pass, and — the real test — says which 10 constructs you would order first and why, given a fixed budget — and, since this is where design stops being hypothetical, names the screening and ordering controls that apply to a synthesis order (see the biosecurity subsection of [Section 11](../11-frontiers/README.md)). Success criterion: at least one design clears scRMSD < 2 Å, pLDDT > 80 and interface PAE < 10; your review correctly diagnoses the main failure mode in the rejects; and your memo states at least one way your own filtering could be systematically fooling you. *(~15 h)*
