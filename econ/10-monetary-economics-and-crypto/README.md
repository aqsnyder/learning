# Monetary Economics and Crypto

## Why This Section Matters

Money is the most fundamental economic technology — it solves the double coincidence of wants problem, serves as a unit of account, and stores value across time. Understanding the economics of money, banking, and monetary policy is essential for making sense of inflation, interest rates, financial crises, and central bank behavior. The emergence of Bitcoin and cryptocurrency has added an entirely new dimension: programmable money, decentralized consensus, token economics, and DeFi create novel economic systems that can be analyzed with the tools from earlier sections (mechanism design, game theory, information economics). This section bridges traditional monetary theory with the economics of crypto. [Section 14](../14-macroeconomics-money-and-financial-markets/README.md) supplies the money-and-banking base it assumes. The mainstream analyses listed here (Budish; Halaburda et al.; Liu, Makarov & Schoar; the BIS) are the counterweights to read against the advocacy texts.

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **What is money? Yap vs. stock-to-flow** — (a) In 300 words each, steelman Martin's claim that money is transferable credit (his account of Yap's stone money) and Ammous's claim that money is the most salable commodity; end each with one observable prediction that would distinguish them. (b) From the issuance schedule (50 BTC per block, halving every 210,000 blocks, ≈ 52,560 blocks/year), compute Bitcoin's supply and stock-to-flow ratio by year through 2040 and plot them. (c) Read what Hayek's competing issuers would promise in *Denationalisation of Money* — stable purchasing power, not a fixed quantity — and contrast it with Ammous: which property should a medium of exchange optimize? **Check:** after the 2024 halving, supply ≈ 19.69M BTC, flow ≈ 164k BTC/year, S2F ≈ 120; total supply approaches but never reaches 21M. *(~3 h · Martin, Money: The Unauthorized Biography; Ammous, The Bitcoin Standard; Hayek, Denationalisation of Money)*
2. **Nakamoto's attacker calculation, checked** — (a) Port the whitepaper's C function for the attacker's success probability to Python and reproduce its tables, including the z needed for P < 0.1% at q = 0.10 … 0.45. (b) Simulate the race directly: while the honest chain mines z blocks, the attacker's progress is negative-binomial, not Poisson — compare. (c) Turn it into policy: for attacker share q and order value V, how many confirmations before you ship a $3,000 instrument paid on-chain, at 0.1% vs. 0.01% acceptable risk? **Check:** you match the paper exactly (q = 0.1, z = 5 → 0.0009137; q = 0.45 needs z = 340); the exact race gives ≈ 0.0018 at q = 0.1, z = 5 — about double — because the paper's approximation treats the time for z honest blocks as fixed. *(~2.5 h · Nakamoto, "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System"; Gensler, Blockchain and Money (MIT), lectures on consensus)*
3. **Security budget and Budish's critique** — (a) Project miner revenue per block (subsidy + fees) through 2040 under three fee scenarios, in BTC and in USD along a few price paths. (b) Budish's core argument ("The Economic Limits of Bitcoin and the Blockchain", listed above): free entry makes miners' total per-block cost equal the per-block payment p_block, so an attacker who must out-hash the network for t blocks pays about A·t·p_block (A ≥ 1), and the chain is safe only if the one-off attack value V_attack < A·t·p_block. Derive that inequality from those two steps, then compute the implied maximum safe value at risk per block under your projections. (c) Write a 300-word critique: which assumptions (rentable hash rate, whether attack costs are recoverable, what a crashing coin is worth to an attacker) are strongest and weakest, and how Ammous would respond. **Check:** you can state why, in Budish's model, security scales with the *flow* paid to miners rather than with the coin's market capitalization. *(~3 h · Budish, "The Economic Limits of Bitcoin and the Blockchain"; Nakamoto, section on incentives; Ammous; Halaburda et al. for the mainstream context)*
4. **Rules vs. discretion with real data** — (a) Derive the Barro–Gordon result: with Lucas supply y = y* + a(π − πᵉ) and loss ½π² + ½λ(y − ky*)² with k > 1, solve for discretionary inflation under rational expectations and compare it with commitment. (b) Pull PCE inflation, the output gap (CBO potential GDP), and the effective fed funds rate from FRED (Section 14), and plot the Taylor (1993) rule i = π + 0.5·gap + 0.5(π − 2) + 2 against the actual rate since 1990. (c) Pick the three largest deviations and argue in 200 words whether each was "discretion" in the Barro–Gordon sense or a defensible response to something the rule ignores. **Check:** discretionary inflation = λa(k − 1)y* > 0 with no output gain; the rule called for far higher rates than actual in 2021–22. *(~3 h · Walsh, Monetary Theory and Policy, chapters on discretion and time inconsistency and on policy rules; Dalio's video (Section 00) for the credit-cycle backdrop)*
5. **Run on an algorithmic stablecoin** — Build a small agent-based model of a Terra-style design: a stablecoin redeemable for $1 worth of a volatile "share" token minted on demand. Holders redeem when they expect the peg to fail; redemptions mint share tokens and push their price down; a falling share price raises everyone's expectation of failure. (a) Find the redemption fraction that tips the system into a death spiral. (b) Compare with a fully reserved stablecoin and with a 40% external reserve. (c) Map the model onto a two-equilibrium run/don't-run coordination game (Section 02), and compare the design with the stable-value hedging contract in the "financial derivatives and stable-value currencies" part of the Ethereum whitepaper. Then check your model's dynamics against who ran first, and who lost most, in the 2022 Terra crash (Liu, Makarov & Schoar). **Check:** the algorithmic design shows two stable outcomes (peg holds, collapse) separated by a threshold that moves as reserve backing grows; full reserves remove the bad equilibrium only if redemptions are honored. *(~4 h · Buterin, Ethereum whitepaper; Liu, Makarov & Schoar, "Anatomy of a Run"; Walsh on money demand; Section 02 coordination games; Section 14's Diamond–Dybvig model)*
