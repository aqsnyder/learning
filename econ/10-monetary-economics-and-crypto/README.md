# Monetary Economics and Crypto

## Why This Section Matters

Money is the most fundamental economic technology: it solves the double coincidence of wants, serves as a unit of account, and stores value across time. This section teaches monetary economics first and crypto second.

- **Part 1: money, banking and central banking.** What money is (an IOU that everyone trusts), who creates each of its forms, why a bank deposit is worth exactly as much as a banknote, and what central banks do: set short-term interest rates, lend as a last resort in a panic, and choose between rules and discretion.
- **Part 2: crypto, read as monetary economics.** Bitcoin, Ethereum and stablecoins tested with Part 1's questions and with the tools of earlier sections (mechanism design, game theory, information economics): is bitcoin money, what secures it, and why do stablecoins run?

[Section 14](../14-macroeconomics-money-and-financial-markets/README.md) supplies the mechanics Part 1 assumes: money creation in T-accounts and the Diamond–Dybvig model of bank runs. The btc curriculum goes deeper on the protocol itself; the links are under Part 2's resources. The mainstream analyses listed here (the Bank of England, Bernanke, Halaburda et al., Budish, Gorton & Zhang, Liu, Makarov & Schoar, the BIS) are the counterweights to read against the advocacy texts.

**Educational only:** nothing in this section is investment advice.

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:
- Explain what money is and who issues each of its three modern forms (currency, bank deposits, central-bank reserves), and say what keeps a bank deposit or a stablecoin at par with central-bank money.
- Explain a central bank's two missions and two tools (interest-rate policy and lending of last resort), derive the Barro–Gordon inflation bias of discretionary policy, and compute a Taylor-rule policy rate from FRED data.
- Compare the commodity and credit views of money (Ammous vs. Martin, with Hayek's competing currencies) and test "salability" and stock-to-flow claims against Bitcoin's actual issuance schedule.
- Reproduce Nakamoto's double-spend probability and turn it into a confirmation policy, and explain proof-of-work security as an economic budget (Budish's flow-vs-stock argument) projected across future halvings.
- Model a stablecoin run as a coordination game with two equilibria, identify what makes a peg robust, and judge stablecoin designs against the BIS tests and the history of private banknotes.

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **What is money? Yap vs. stock-to-flow** — (a) Using the Bank of England's framework, write the balance-sheet entries (whose asset, whose liability) for five things people call money: a banknote, a deposit at a commercial bank, a commercial bank's reserve balance at the central bank, a balance in a fiat-backed stablecoin, and a bitcoin. For each, say what keeps it at par with central-bank money and who stands behind that promise. (b) In 250 words each, steelman Martin's claim that money is transferable credit (his account of Yap's stone money) and Ammous's claim that money is the most salable commodity; end each with one observable prediction that would distinguish them. (c) From the issuance schedule (50 BTC per block, halving every 210,000 blocks, ≈ 52,560 blocks/year), compute Bitcoin's supply and stock-to-flow ratio by year through 2040 and plot them. (d) Read what Hayek's competing issuers would promise in *Denationalisation of Money* — stable purchasing power, not a fixed quantity — and contrast it with Ammous: which property should a medium of exchange optimize? **Check:** (a) the banknote and reserves are central-bank liabilities, and only banks hold reserves; the deposit is the bank's liability, held at par by the promise to convert it into central-bank money on demand, backed by deposit insurance and the lender of last resort; the stablecoin is its issuer's liability, at par only while redemptions are honored from the issuer's reserve assets; a bitcoin is no one's liability. (c) After the 2024 halving, supply ≈ 19.69M BTC, flow ≈ 164k BTC/year, S2F ≈ 120; total supply approaches but never reaches 21M. *(~3.5 h · McLeay, Radia & Thomas, "Money in the modern economy: an introduction"; Section 14's "Money creation in the modern economy"; Martin, Money: The Unauthorised Biography; Ammous, The Bitcoin Standard; Hayek, Denationalisation of Money)*
2. **Nakamoto's attacker calculation, checked** — (a) Port the whitepaper's C function for the attacker's success probability to Python and reproduce its tables, including the z needed for P < 0.1% at q = 0.10 … 0.45. (b) Simulate the race directly: while the honest chain mines z blocks, the attacker's progress is negative-binomial, not Poisson — compare. (c) Turn it into policy: for attacker share q and order value V, how many confirmations before you ship a $3,000 instrument paid on-chain, at 0.1% vs. 0.01% acceptable risk? **Check:** you match the paper exactly (q = 0.1, z = 5 → 0.0009137; q = 0.45 needs z = 340); the exact race gives ≈ 0.0018 at q = 0.1, z = 5 — about double — because the paper's approximation treats the time for z honest blocks as fixed. *(~2.5 h · Nakamoto, "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System"; Halaburda et al., section on the consensus mechanism; Gensler, Blockchain and Money (MIT), lectures on consensus)*
3. **Security budget and Budish's critique** — (a) Project miner revenue per block (subsidy + fees) through 2040 under three fee scenarios, in BTC and in USD along a few price paths. (b) Budish's core argument ("The Economic Limits of Bitcoin and the Blockchain", listed above): free entry makes miners' total per-block cost equal the per-block payment p_block, so an attacker who must out-hash the network for t blocks pays about A·t·p_block (A ≥ 1), and the chain is safe only if the one-off attack value V_attack < A·t·p_block. Derive that inequality from those two steps, then compute the implied maximum safe value at risk per block under your projections. (c) Write a 300-word critique: which assumptions (rentable hash rate, whether attack costs are recoverable, what a crashing coin is worth to an attacker) are strongest and weakest, and how Ammous would respond. **Check:** you can state why, in Budish's model, security scales with the *flow* paid to miners rather than with the coin's market capitalization. *(~3 h · Budish, "The Economic Limits of Bitcoin and the Blockchain" (btc/09); Halaburda et al., the flow-vs-stock attack model; Nakamoto, section on incentives; Ammous)*
4. **Rules vs. discretion with real data** — (a) Derive the Barro–Gordon result: with Lucas supply y = y* + a(π − πᵉ) and loss ½π² + ½λ(y − ky*)² with k > 1, solve for discretionary inflation under rational expectations and compare it with commitment. (b) Pull PCE inflation, the output gap (CBO potential GDP), and the effective fed funds rate from FRED (Section 14), and plot the Taylor (1993) rule i = π + 0.5·gap + 0.5(π − 2) + 2 against the actual rate since 1990. (c) Pick the three largest deviations and argue in 200 words whether each was "discretion" in the Barro–Gordon sense or a defensible response to something the rule ignores, such as a financial panic. (d) The second tool: from Bernanke's first lecture, state Bagehot's dictum and explain in 150 words why a central bank that lends freely in a panic can stop a run that its interest-rate tool cannot, and who, if anyone, could play that role for a stablecoin issuer. **Check:** discretionary inflation = λa(k − 1)y* > 0 with no output gain; the rule called for far higher rates than actual in 2021–22; (d) names all three conditions (lend freely, against good assets, at a penalty rate) and says the penalty rate is there to discourage excessive use. *(~3.5 h · Walsh, Monetary Theory and Policy, chapters on discretion and time inconsistency and on policy rules; Taylor, "Discretion versus policy rules in practice"; Bernanke's lectures 1 and 3; FRED (Section 14))*
5. **Run on an algorithmic stablecoin** — Build a small agent-based model of a Terra-style design: a stablecoin redeemable for $1 worth of a volatile "share" token minted on demand. Holders redeem when they expect the peg to fail; redemptions mint share tokens and push their price down; a falling share price raises everyone's expectation of failure. (a) Find the redemption fraction that tips the system into a death spiral. (b) Compare with a fully reserved stablecoin and with a 40% external reserve. (c) Map the model onto a two-equilibrium run/don't-run coordination game (Section 02), and compare the design with the stable-value hedging contract in the "financial derivatives and stable-value currencies" part of the Ethereum whitepaper. Then check your model's dynamics against who ran first, and who lost most, in the 2022 Terra crash (Liu, Makarov & Schoar). (d) Judge your three designs against the BIS tests (singleness, elasticity, integrity) and Gorton & Zhang's comparison with the private banknotes of the 1800s: which design, if any, would be accepted at face value without questions, and what would it take? **Check:** the algorithmic design shows two stable outcomes (peg holds, collapse) separated by a threshold that moves as reserve backing grows; full reserves remove the bad equilibrium only if redemptions are honored; (d) names, for each design, the test it fails and the institution (deposit insurance, a lender of last resort, bank regulation) that keeps a bank deposit at par. *(~4.5 h · Buterin, Ethereum whitepaper; Liu, Makarov & Schoar, "Anatomy of a Run"; Gorton & Zhang, "Taming Wildcat Stablecoins"; BIS Annual Economic Report 2025, Chapter III; Section 02 coordination games; Section 14's Diamond–Dybvig model)*
