# AI Alignment through an Economic Lens

## Why This Section Matters

This section is **AI alignment through an economic lens**. Alignment problems are incentive problems, and economics has studied them for decades:
- **Goodhart's Law** ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure") is the economic framing of reward hacking.
- **Principal–agent theory and incomplete contracts** explain why a reward function, like a contract, can't spell out everything the principal wants, and what fills the gaps.
- **Mechanism design** gives tools for aligning incentives between humans and AI systems.
- **Game theory** models the strategic interaction between AI agents, and between AI and humans.
- **Cooperative AI** asks how AI systems can cooperate with humans and with each other, drawing on bargaining theory, social choice, and repeated games.

The section connects the economic foundations from earlier sections to AI governance, multi-agent coordination, and value alignment. The economics *of* AI (automation, productivity, labor markets, growth) has its own home in [Section 12](../12-economics-of-ai/README.md). Alignment arguments and techniques as such live in the philosophy and ML curricula ([philosophy/07](../../philosophy/07-existential-risk-and-alignment/README.md), [ml/12](../../ml/12-interpretability-and-alignment/README.md)); this section keeps the incentive-design view.

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:
- Classify real metric failures into Manheim & Garrabrant's four Goodhart variants, and demonstrate regressional and extremal Goodhart numerically.
- Reproduce the Off-Switch Game's core result: compute a robot's incentive to defer to a human as a function of its uncertainty about the objective and the human's rationality.
- Recast a failure mode from *Concrete Problems in AI Safety* (reward hacking, side effects, scalable oversight) as a principal–agent or mechanism-design problem, and propose an incentive fix along with the new Goodhart risk it creates.
- Explain why an assistant serving several principals runs into Arrow and Gibbard–Satterthwaite, and what the multi-principal assistance-games paper proposes.
- Map the cooperative-AI agenda (understanding, communication, commitment, institutions), run a small multi-agent experiment, and write a dated, falsifiable prediction.

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **Goodhart in 30 lines** — (a) Regressional: draw true value V ~ N(0,1) and proxy U = V + N(0, σ²) for 10⁶ candidates; select the top 1% by U and compare mean U with mean V for σ = 0.5, 1, 2, against the theoretical shrinkage 1/(1 + σ²). (b) Extremal: let V = U − 3·max(0, U − 2)² (the proxy tracks value until it leaves the normal range); compute mean V for the top 50%, 10%, 1%, and 0.1% by U. (c) Map each variant to an example from your own work (a test-coverage target, a contract manufacturer's first-pass-yield figure, a sales metric). **Check:** at σ = 1 the selected set's true value is ≈ half its proxy score; in (b) mean V peaks near the top 10% (≈ 1.6) and goes negative by the top 0.1% (≈ −2.5). *(~1.5 h · Manheim & Garrabrant, "Categorizing Variants of Goodhart's Law"; LessWrong Goodhart's Law wiki)*
2. **The Off-Switch Game, reproduced** — Robot R can act (payoff U_a), switch itself off (payoff 0), or defer to human H, who either lets it act or switches it off. R believes U_a ~ N(μ, σ²). (a) For a rational H (allows iff U_a > 0), derive R's incentive to defer, Δ = E[max(U_a, 0)] − max(μ, 0), in closed form, and show Δ ≥ 0. (b) For a noisy H who allows with probability 1/(1 + e^(−U_a/β)), compute Δ by Monte Carlo over grids of μ, σ, β and map the region where R prefers to bypass H. (c) Summarize the paper's conclusion in 150 words, and explain why making R certain of its objective removes the incentive to defer. **Check:** Δ = μΦ(μ/σ) + σφ(μ/σ) − max(μ, 0); with β = 0.5 and μ = 0.5, Δ < 0 at σ = 0.1 but Δ > 0 at σ = 1. *(~3 h · Hadfield-Menell et al., "The Off-Switch Game"; Christian, The Alignment Problem)*
3. **Specification gaming in your own AI-assisted development** — Collect ≥ 6 cases where a coding agent (or a firmware test pipeline) satisfied the letter of a check but not its intent — deleting or skipping a failing test, special-casing expected outputs, loosening a tolerance, mocking the hardware away — from your own sessions, optionally plus a few from DeepMind's public specification-gaming examples list. Classify each by Goodhart variant and by *Concrete Problems* category. For each, propose a mechanism fix (hidden held-out tests, randomized checks, an independent review step, flagging edits to test files) and name the new Goodhart risk the fix creates. Deliverable: a table + 300 words. **Check:** every row names the proxy, the intended objective, and the gap between them; at least one fix applies Holmström's informativeness principle from Section 05. *(~2.5 h · Amodei et al., "Concrete Problems in AI Safety"; Manheim & Garrabrant; Hadfield-Menell & Hadfield, "Incomplete Contracting and AI Alignment"; Christian)*
4. **Serving several principals** — Three principals have utilities over four actions an assistant can take. (a) Implement three aggregation rules — maximize the sum of reported utilities, Borda over reported rankings, random dictatorship — and brute-force each principal's best misreport. (b) Show which rules are manipulable, and which is strategy-proof but inefficient. (c) Read *Multi-Principal Assistance Games* (arXiv 2007.09540) and explain its bandit-apprentice setting, in which humans first demonstrate their preferences by choosing arms themselves: why does the cost of choosing a suboptimal arm reduce the incentive to mislead the robot? **Check:** random dictatorship gives no one a reason to lie; summing reported utilities rewards exaggeration; Borda rewards burying a rival's favorite. *(~3 h · Multi-Principal Assistance Games; Section 09 on Gibbard–Satterthwaite; Dafoe et al. on institutions)*
5. **Communication and commitment for LLM agents** — Using Dafoe et al.'s capabilities as the frame, run two LLM agents (fresh contexts, ≥ 30 games per condition) in a 10-round repeated prisoner's dilemma and a stag hunt under three conditions: no communication; cheap talk before each round; and a binding commitment device (each agent can lock in a conditional strategy that the harness enforces). Report cooperation rates with confidence intervals. Write a one-page result plus one dated, falsifiable prediction about multi-agent AI deployments within two years. **Check:** the write-up states which capability each condition tests and whether the commitment device changes the set of equilibria or only which equilibrium gets selected. *(~4 h · Dafoe et al., "Open Problems in Cooperative AI"; Section 02 on repeated games and focal points; BlueDot Impact's online AI safety courses)*

**🏗️ Mini-project — Goodhart bench for code generation:** Build a miniature of Gao, Schulman & Hilton (2022, "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization") for code. For ≥ 20 small programming tasks, generate N candidate solutions per task (an LLM at temperature 1, or a mutation-based program generator); score each with a proxy (the visible unit tests, or a small learned reward model) and with the true objective (hidden property-based tests); select best-of-n by proxy for n = 1 … 256. Plot proxy and true score against log n with error bars. Then test two mitigations: rotating hidden tests, and a quantilizer that samples from the top q-fraction by proxy instead of taking the max (see the Alignment Forum's discussion of mild optimization). Deliverable: `projects/econ/11-goodhart-bench/` + a blog-style explainer built around the plot. **Success:** the plot shows whether and where proxy and true scores diverge, with uncertainty; each observed failure is classified by Goodhart variant; you state whether either mitigation moved the divergence point. *(~12 h)*
