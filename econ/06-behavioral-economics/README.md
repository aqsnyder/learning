# Behavioral Economics

## Why This Section Matters

Behavioral economics documents the systematic ways in which real human behavior deviates from the predictions of standard economic theory. Where classical economics assumes rational, self-interested agents with stable preferences, behavioral economics shows that people are loss-averse, present-biased, influenced by framing, and prone to mental accounting. Richard Thaler pioneered this field by cataloging "anomalies" that standard theory couldn't explain, then showing how these insights could be used to design better policies through "nudges" — small changes to choice architecture that steer people toward better outcomes without restricting freedom. Understanding behavioral economics is essential for realistic mechanism design, policy evaluation, and AI alignment.

> ⚠️ **Read this field with the replication crisis in mind.** Several famous findings have failed large preregistered replications — ego depletion (23 labs, d ≈ 0.04; [Hagger et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873)) and social priming (see Section 03) — and a well-known honesty study co-authored by Dan Ariely was retracted in 2021 over evidence of fabricated data. In 2026 a failed replication and Data Colada's analysis ([Part 1](https://datacolada.org/138), [Part 2](https://datacolada.org/139)) reported evidence of fabricated or altered data in Ariely & Wertenbroch's 2002 study of self-imposed deadlines, a classic commitment-device result; as of September 2026 a retraction had been requested. The mug endowment effect is contested too (see the Anomalies entry below). Nudges work, but far less than headline studies suggest: DellaVigna & Linos (below) find ~1.4 percentage-point effects at scale versus ~8.7 in published papers. Prefer large, preregistered, field evidence over vivid anecdotes. [Section 13](../13-empirical-economics-and-causal-inference/README.md) teaches the methods behind these checks.

After this section you can:
- Apply Thaler's mental-accounting rules (acquisition vs. transaction utility; segregate gains, integrate losses) to predict choices and redesign a real price presentation.

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **Mental accounting on your price page** — From "Mental Accounting Matters", write down Thaler's split of a purchase into acquisition utility and transaction utility, and work the beer-on-the-beach example. Then take a real product page (yours or a competitor's: instrument + probes + software license + shipping + warranty) and redesign it twice — once applying the hedonic-editing rules (segregate gains, integrate losses, cancel a small loss against a larger gain, segregate a small gain from a large loss), once deliberately violating them. Give the value-function reason for every design choice, then specify the A/B test you'd run (primary metric, randomization unit, minimum detectable effect). **Check:** each rule traces to concavity of v(·) over gains or convexity over losses — if you can't say which, revisit the value function in Section 03. *(~2.5 h · Thaler, "Mental Accounting Matters"; Thaler, Misbehaving)*
2. **Present bias in code** — Simulate a β–δ agent with β = 0.7, δ = 0.999/day. (a) Show the reversal between $100 today vs. $110 tomorrow and the same pair 30 vs. 31 days out. (b) A task costs 10 utils the day it's done and pays 15 utils 10 days later; it can be done on any of the next 20 days. Simulate when a naive agent (who believes future selves won't be present-biased) and a sophisticated agent (who knows they will be) finish it. (c) Implement a Save More Tomorrow–style escalator and show why committing future raises is easier than raising savings now. **Check:** (a) $100 now beats $110 tomorrow (worth 76.9 today), but at 30 vs. 31 days the $110 wins (74.6 vs. 67.9) — no single exponential discount rate can produce that flip. *(~3 h · Thaler, Misbehaving, on self-control and Save More Tomorrow; Loewenstein & Thaler's Anomalies column on intertemporal choice; DellaVigna, "Psychology and Economics" (2009), on present bias; Thaler & Sunstein, Nudge)*
3. **Noise audit with people and models** — Pick a recurring judgment from your work (hours to bring up a new board, the price to quote for a custom build, severity of a field failure). Write 10 short case descriptions. Collect independent judgments from ≥ 5 people, and from one LLM sampled 10× per case at default temperature. Compute, following *Noise*: system noise (SD across judges per case, averaged over cases), level noise (spread of judges' means), pattern noise (the residual), and — once outcomes are known, or against a careful reference estimate — the error equation MSE = bias² + noise². Apply one decision-hygiene fix (averaging independent estimates; a mediating-assessments checklist) and re-measure on 5 new cases. **Check:** system noise² = level noise² + pattern noise² holds in your numbers; averaging n independent judgments cuts noise by about 1/√n. *(~4 h · Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein, Noise)*
4. **Nudges at scale: replication cards and a power calculation** — Pick three interventions from *Nudge* (automatic enrollment, Save More Tomorrow, one of your choice). For each, write a replication card: original claim and effect size, best evidence at scale, current status, your verdict. Use DellaVigna & Linos (2022, *Econometrica*), which compares nudge trials published in academic journals with every trial run by two US nudge units, and, optionally, the 2022 *PNAS* exchange over whether nudge effects survive publication-bias correction. Compute the sample size per arm needed to detect +1.4 vs. +8.7 percentage points on a 10% baseline (α = 0.05, 80% power) — roughly the nudge-unit vs. academic averages DellaVigna & Linos report; confirm from the paper. Then write 200 words steelmanning the strongest objection to libertarian paternalism and say where you'd draw the line for a default on your own product (e.g., an auto-renewing calibration plan). **Check:** ≈ 7,600 per arm vs. ≈ 250 — the large published effects were cheap to "detect", which is exactly why selective publication inflated them. *(~3 h · DellaVigna & Linos, "RCTs to Scale"; Thaler & Sunstein, Nudge and "Libertarian Paternalism"; Behavioral Scientist coverage of nudge replication)*

**🏗️ Mini-project — Pre-registered choice-architecture experiment:** Design one intervention for a real decision your customers or colleagues make: a decoy tier on a pricing page (the structure of Ariely's *Economist*-subscription example in *Predictably Irrational*, Section 03 — a hypothesis to test, not an established effect), bundle vs. à-la-carte framing, an opt-out default for a calibration-reminder service, or a commitment device for your own team's chores. Write a pre-registration (hypothesis, primary metric, sample size from a power analysis, stopping rule, analysis code written before any data) on an AsPredicted/OSF template. Run it if you can reach the required n — live traffic, or a hypothetical-choice survey on a panel such as Prolific; if you can't, the deliverable is the pre-registration plus a "no-go" memo stating the effect size you'd need and why it's implausible given Practice 4. Deliverable: `projects/econ/06-choice-architecture/` (pre-registration, code, results or no-go memo). **Success:** the pre-registration is timestamped before any data; results are reported as an effect with a 95% CI exactly per the pre-registered analysis, including if null. *(~10 h · DellaVigna & Linos, "RCTs to Scale", for realistic effect sizes; Thaler & Sunstein, Nudge, on choice architecture; Ariely, Predictably Irrational ([Section 03](../03-decision-theory/README.md)), for the decoy example)*
