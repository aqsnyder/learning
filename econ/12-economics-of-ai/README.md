# Economics of AI

## Why This Section Matters

Generative AI is a general-purpose technology whose economic effects are being measured almost as fast as it spreads. The questions are concrete for anyone who runs a company or develops with AI assistance: which tasks get cheaper, by how much, and for whom; what that does to wages, hiring, and the share of income going to labor; and whether it adds a rounding error or a new growth regime to GDP. Economists answer them with a small set of tools:
- **Frameworks.** The task-based model of automation (displacement, productivity and reinstatement effects), and the view of AI as a drop in the cost of prediction.
- **Micro evidence.** Randomized and quasi-experimental studies of writing, customer support, consulting and software work, including null and negative results.
- **Exposure and usage measures.** Which occupations' tasks an LLM *could* speed up, and which tasks people actually use it for.
- **Labor-market outcomes.** Early evidence on earnings, hours and hiring.
- **Macro estimates and scenarios.** From a few tenths of a percent of TFP over a decade to scenarios in which wages collapse or boom.

The evidence is early and conflicting, so this section deliberately pairs optimistic and skeptical estimates. Judge each study by its design, population and outcome, using the tools in [Section 13](../13-empirical-economics-and-causal-inference/README.md), before you quote its headline number. Section 11 covers the *alignment* side of AI through an economic lens; this section covers the economics *of* AI.

**Where it fits:** this section is numbered 12 only because it was added after the original sections. Take it after Section 11, with Section 13 before or alongside it.

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:
- Use the task-based framework to separate an automation technology's displacement, productivity and reinstatement effects, and show numerically how automation that firms find profitable can raise output while lowering wages.
- Reproduce Acemoglu's Hulten-style estimate of AI's ten-year TFP effect from its inputs, and explain why it sits so far below transformative-AI scenarios such as Korinek & Suh's.
- Read the main generative-AI productivity studies as an econometrician — design, estimand, population, effect size — and explain how a 56% speed-up and a 19% slow-down can both be credible.
- Distinguish exposure measures (Eloundou et al.'s α, β, ζ), usage measures (the Anthropic Economic Index) and realized labor-market outcomes, and say what each can and cannot tell you.
- Design and power-analyze a small randomized evaluation of AI assistance in your own workflow, stating its minimum detectable effect before you run it.

An AI assistant rolled out to 5,179 customer-support agents raised issues resolved per hour by 14% on average and by 34% for novice and low-skilled agents, with minimal impact on experienced, highly skilled agents. The most-cited field evidence. |

A randomized experiment with 453 college-educated professionals doing midlevel, occupation-specific writing tasks: access to ChatGPT cut average time by 40% and raised quality by 18%, and inequality between workers decreased. |

On 18 tasks inside the AI "frontier", AI users completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with higher quality. On a task chosen to lie outside it, they were less likely to get the right answer. The source of the "jagged frontier" metaphor. |
| | 📄 Paper | The Impact of AI on Developer Productivity: Evidence from GitHub Copilot | Sida Peng, Eirini Kalliamvakou, Peter Cihon & Mert Demirer | 2023 | [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590) | A controlled experiment on a single, well-specified programming task: the group with Copilot finished 55.8% faster. | | 📄 Paper | Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity | Joel Becker, Nate Rush, Elizabeth Barnes & David Rein | 2025 | [Link](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089) | METR's randomized trial: 16 experienced open-source developers and 246 tasks. The skeptical anchor, and a lesson that self-reports are not measurements. Also in software/09, with the 2026 follow-up. |
| | 📄 Paper | Still Waters, Rapid Currents: Early Labor Market Transformation under Generative AI | Anders Humlum & Emilie Vestergaard | 2025 | [Link](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33777) | NBER WP 33777; first circulated in May 2025 as "Large Language Models, Small Labor Market Effects" (BFI WP 2025-56). Danish survey data linked to administrative records, with difference-in-differences: precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours, ruling out effects larger than 2% two years after ChatGPT's launch. Adoption was nonetheless widespread, and work is being reorganized around new tasks (AI oversight and integration). The skeptical labor-market anchor. |
| | 📄 Paper | Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence | Erik Brynjolfsson, Bharat Chandar & Ruyu Chen | 2025 (rev. 2026) | [Link](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine/) | Stanford Digital Economy Lab, ADP payroll data. There is no economy-wide displacement, but 22–25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations fell behind less-exposed peers (19% below trend in the August 2026 revision). The gap comes through reduced hiring, concentrated where AI substitutes for rather than complements workers. The authors call these descriptive, not causal: the pattern weakens with education controls, and some trends predate generative AI. Read next to Humlum & Vestergaard. |
| 🔑 | 📄 Paper | The Simple Macroeconomics of AI | Daron Acemoglu | 2024 | [Link](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487) | NBER WP 32487, free (published in *Economic Policy* 40(121), 2025). A Hulten-style aggregation of task-level cost savings: TFP rises no more than 0.66% over ten years, and below 0.53% once harder-to-learn tasks are allowed for. Finds no evidence that AI reduces labor-income inequality. The skeptical macro anchor. |
| | 📄 Paper | Scenarios for the Transition to AGI | Anton Korinek & Donghyun Suh | 2024 | [Link](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32255) | NBER WP 32255. A task-complexity model of the road to AGI. Wages keep rising if automation is slow, collapse if every task of bounded complexity is automated, and can fall even before full automation if automation is fast; scarce, irreproducible factors make the declines worse. The counterweight to Acemoglu: what changes if AI keeps improving. |

## Suggested Order

**Core path (🔑):** Acemoglu & Restrepo, "Automation and New Tasks" → Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, "Generative AI at Work" → Acemoglu, "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI"

1. **Acemoglu & Restrepo — "Automation and New Tasks"** — Start with the framework: displacement, productivity and reinstatement effects.
2. **Agrawal, Gans & Goldfarb — "The Ambiguous Labor Market Impact of Automating Prediction"** — Add the prediction lens from the same *JEP* symposium.
3. **Eloundou et al. — "GPTs are GPTs"** — Learn how exposure is measured, and what it is not.
4. **Handa et al. — Anthropic Economic Index** — Compare potential exposure with observed usage.
5. **Noy & Zhang; Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond; Dell'Acqua et al.** — The core micro evidence: writing, customer support, consulting. Note who gains most in each.
6. **Peng et al., then Becker et al. (METR)** — Read the optimistic and the skeptical software study back to back.
7. **Humlum & Vestergaard, then Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen** — Labor-market outcomes, read as a pair: precise nulls on earnings and hours vs. an early-career hiring gap.
8. **Acemoglu — "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI"** — Aggregate the micro evidence into a TFP number.
9. **Korinek & Suh — "Scenarios for the Transition to AGI"** — Then ask what that number leaves out.
10. **Cross-links** — AI agents as simulated economic actors (Horton, Filippas & Manning in [Section 08](../08-computational-economics/README.md)); algorithmic pricing ([Section 08](../08-computational-economics/README.md), [Section 15](../15-industrial-organization-and-strategy/README.md)); alignment as incentive design ([Section 11](../11-ai-alignment-economics-and-frontiers/README.md)); the developer-productivity follow-up in software/09.

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **Displacement vs. reinstatement in a task model** — Tasks x ∈ [N−1, N] are combined Cobb–Douglas, Y = exp(∫ ln y(x) dx). Capital can perform tasks x ≤ I with productivity 1; labor performs any task with productivity e^x; K = L = 1 and N = 1. (a) Show that each task gets an equal share of spending, so the labor share is N − I. Derive ln Y, W = (N − I)·Y/L and R = (I − N + 1)·Y/K. (b) Derive d ln W/dI = ln π(I) − 1/(N − I), where π(I) = (W/e^I)/R is the cost saving from moving the marginal task to capital. (c) Compute Y, W, the labor share and π at I = 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, find where firms stop automating (π = 1), and find the I that maximizes W. (d) Reinstatement: at I = 0.4 raise N to 1.1 (new labor tasks appear at the top and the lowest task drops out), and recompute. (e) In 200 words, map each term onto Acemoglu & Restrepo's displacement, productivity and reinstatement effects, and explain why automation that saves firms very little (π just above 1, "so-so" automation) is the worst case for wages. Then say how the picture changes if AI instead cheapens a *prediction* that feeds a human judgment task (Agrawal, Gans & Goldfarb). **Check:** W ≈ 2.13, 2.03, 1.79 at I = 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 (labor share 0.8, 0.7, 0.6) while Y rises from 2.67 to 2.98; π(0.4) ≈ 1.005, so firms stop at I ≈ 0.401; W peaks near I ≈ 0.19; raising N to 1.1 lifts W to ≈ 2.18 and the labor share to 0.7. *(~3 h · Acemoglu & Restrepo, "Automation and New Tasks"; Agrawal, Gans & Goldfarb; sympy/numpy)*
2. **Acemoglu's back-of-envelope, rebuilt** — (a) From "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI", extract the inputs to the Hulten calculation: the share of tasks exposed, the share of those profitably affected within ten years, the average cost saving per task, and how task-level savings scale to GDP. Reproduce the headline 0.66% TFP gain over ten years and the hard-task-adjusted figure (below 0.53%). (b) Build a sensitivity table that swaps in the exposure measures α, β, ζ from Eloundou et al. and the savings implied by Noy & Zhang (−40% time), Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (+14% output per hour) and Becker et al. (+19% time). Convert each to a cost saving and state your conversion. (c) In 300 words, say what Korinek & Suh's scenarios capture that a Hulten calculation cannot: capital accumulation, a moving automation frontier, and scarce factors. **Check:** you match 0.66% to two significant figures; in the Hulten approximation the gain is linear in each input, so doubling the cost saving doubles it; 0.66% over ten years is ≈ 0.07 percentage points a year. Compare that with the claims you read in the press. *(~3 h · Acemoglu; Korinek & Suh; Eloundou et al.; Noy & Zhang; Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond; Becker et al.)*
3. **Six studies, one table** — For Noy & Zhang; Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond; Dell'Acqua et al.; Peng et al.; Becker et al.; and Humlum & Vestergaard, tabulate: design (RCT, staggered rollout, field experiment, difference-in-differences), population, task, outcome, headline effect, who gains most, and the main threat to external validity for an experienced embedded engineer working in a large existing codebase. Then write 400 words reconciling Peng et al.'s 55.8% speed-up with Becker et al.'s 19% slow-down. **Check:** the table reproduces the headline numbers (−40% time and +18% quality; +14% resolutions per hour, +34% for novices; +12.2% tasks and 25.1% faster inside the frontier; 55.8% faster; 19% slower against a forecast 24% faster; earnings and hours effects above 2% ruled out). The reconciliation names at least three differences: task type and specification, developers' experience with the codebase, tool generation, and how the outcome is defined. *(~3 h · the six papers; Section 13 for the designs)*
4. **Exposure vs. usage for your own occupation** — Take O*NET's 20-task list for [Electronics Engineers, Except Computer (17-2072.00)](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/17-2072.00). (a) Rate each task E0/E1/E2 with Eloundou et al.'s rubric, first yourself, then with an LLM given the paper's rubric text; compute α, β and ζ as task shares and your agreement rate with the LLM. (b) From the Anthropic Economic Index data, find how much observed usage maps to this occupation's tasks. (c) In 250 words, explain why exposure (what *could* change) and usage (what one provider's users do) can both differ from realized employment effects (Humlum & Vestergaard; Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen). **Check:** α ≤ β ≤ ζ for any ratings, by construction; you can name the population each measure describes and one reason your self-ratings might be biased. *(~3 h · Eloundou et al.; Handa et al.; the two labor-market papers)*
5. **Your own METR-style trial, powered first** — Pre-register the trial: over about four weeks, before starting each eligible firmware or Python task, write down a time forecast and flip a coin for "AI allowed" vs. "no AI". Record wall-clock time, and later any defects traced back to the task. (a) Before collecting anything, compute the minimum detectable effect, assuming a log-time SD of 0.8 and 15 tasks per arm (α = 0.05, power 0.8). Then compute how many tasks per arm would detect a 20% change. (b) Run the trial, analyze log time with a permutation test, and plot forecast against actual time by arm. (c) Write up what you can and cannot conclude. **Check:** with 15 tasks per arm the MDE is ≈ 0.85 log points, a ≈ 2.3× time ratio (t-test); a 20% effect (0.18 log points) needs ≈ 300 tasks per arm. Treat a 30-task study as a calibration of your own forecasts, not as proof. *(~5 h of analysis spread over 4 weeks · Becker et al.; Section 13 on power and pre-registration)*

**🏗️ Mini-project — AI adoption memo for a small hardware company:** Build a task inventory for a small hardware company: 25–40 tasks with hours per month across design, firmware, test development, documentation, support, and sales/operations. For each task, rate exposure with Practice 4's rubric and choose an evidence-based productivity range. Take the skeptical bound from Becker et al. and Acemoglu and the optimistic one from Peng et al. and Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, and feed in your own trial from Practice 5 where it applies. Compute the change in hours and cost under three scenarios. Then use Practice 1's task model to reason about which roles face displacement and which gain reinstatement through new tasks (AI oversight, evaluation, integration; Humlum & Vestergaard). Deliverable: `projects/econ/12-ai-adoption-memo/`, a notebook plus a 2-page memo with a decision, its hiring implications, and one dated, falsifiable prediction to check in six months. **Success:** every productivity assumption cites a study and that study's population; the memo gives a range bounded by the skeptical and the optimistic evidence rather than a single number; the prediction names a metric, a threshold and a date. *(~10 h)*
