# Economics and Game Theory

> **Why this section matters:** Bitcoin is as much an economic innovation as a technical one. Understanding monetary theory, incentive structures, and game theory gives you the full picture.

**Prerequisites:** Section [00 — History and Philosophy](../00-history-and-philosophy/). For the underlying theory see the economics curriculum: [econ/02 — Game Theory](../../econ/02-game-theory/README.md) and [econ/10 — Monetary Economics and Crypto](../../econ/10-monetary-economics-and-crypto/README.md).

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:
- Derive Bitcoin's supply schedule from consensus code and compute stock-to-flow at each halving.
- Test the Stock-to-Flow model critically: in-sample fit, out-of-sample performance and spurious-regression risk.
- Estimate the security budget and the cost of a majority-hashrate attack under explicit, stated assumptions.
- Model miner incentives — fee sniping, the post-subsidy fee market, selfish mining — with simple payoff calculations.
- Evaluate the monetary arguments in the section's books (Gresham/Thiers, network effects, sound money), separating empirical claims, theory and advocacy.

## Suggested Order

**Core path (🔑):** Budish, "The Economic Limits of Bitcoin and the Blockchain" → Carlsten et al., "On the Instability of Bitcoin Without the Block Reward"

> **Incentive economics first, advocacy last.** Steps 1–4 are the analytical core: how miners are paid and what that payment buys in security. Steps 5–7 add market structure and monetary theory. Steps 8–14 are the advocacy case. Read them as claims to test: log each checkable claim, with its page number or timestamp, in the mini-project's Claims Ledger.

1. **Game theory of mining** — Payoffs, Nash equilibrium and selfish mining (recap Section 03's selfish-mining topic), and how a fee market changes miners' choices.
2. **Budish: The Economic Limits of Bitcoin and the Blockchain** — Why the security budget is an economic constraint: the recurring payment to miners must outweigh the one-off benefit of an attack.
3. **Optech: Fee sniping** — What happens to miners' incentives as the subsidy shrinks toward zero, and the mitigations.
4. **Carlsten et al.: On the Instability of Bitcoin Without the Block Reward** — Undercutting, the mining gap and selfish mining once fees are miners' only income.
5. **SEC statement on spot bitcoin ETPs (2024)** — Market structure: who holds the coins, through whom, and what custodial ownership changes.
6. **Gresham's Law and Thiers' Law** — How monetary history applies to Bitcoin, and which institutional condition decides between the two laws.
7. **Network effects and Metcalfe's law** — Why adoption may compound value, and how contested the empirical evidence is.
8. **The Bitcoin Standard** — The Austrian-school case for bitcoin as money; log its checkable claims.
9. **The Bullish Case for Bitcoin** — Boyapati's investment thesis; separate its empirical claims from its predictions.
10. **Stock-to-Flow model** — The price model these arguments lean on. Test it (Practice 5) rather than trust a fitted curve; the supply schedule itself is in Section 03.
11. **The Fiat Standard** — Ammous's critique of the fiat system; check its historical claims against econ/10.
12. **The Internet of Money** — Antonopoulos's "why it matters" talks; note which claims are about technology and which about money.
13. **The Sovereign Individual** — The 1997 predictions about digital money and the state; score them against what happened.
14. **Michael Saylor Bitcoin talks** — Corporate-treasury and macro advocacy; one talk is enough for the Claims Ledger.

## 🛠️ Practice

*Analysis exercises only — nothing here is investment advice.*

1. **Supply schedule from code** — Reimplement Bitcoin Core's `GetBlockSubsidy` (integer satoshis, one right shift per 210,000 blocks) and sum it to derive the exact maximum supply (it isn't 21,000,000), the height of the last subsidy-paying block, and the approximate year. Compute stock-to-flow at each halving and compare it with the gold and silver figures quoted in *The Bitcoin Standard*. List three reasons the spendable supply is below the schedule (start with the genesis output and BIP 30's duplicate coinbases). *(~1.5 h · The Bitcoin Standard; Stock-to-Flow concept; Mastering Bitcoin ch. 12 and Learn Me A Bitcoin: Block Reward (both Section 03))*
2. **Security-budget scenarios** — Pull per-block fees for the last 2,016 blocks with `getblockstats` (your node must still hold them — raise `prune` if needed — or use mempool.space's API). For the 2028, 2032 and 2036 halvings, tabulate miner revenue per block in BTC under low/medium/high fee scenarios, and express the hashrate it can sustain as a function of price P and an all-in cost per TH-day (keep P symbolic). Estimate what it would cost to rent or build enough hashrate to out-mine the network for six blocks vs for one day, then read that cost through Budish's argument: what one-off attack benefit would it deter? Close with 200 words: what fee level per block would replace today's subsidy, and what would it imply for transaction fees? *(~2.5 h · Game theory of mining concept; Bitcoin Optech: Fee sniping; Budish, The Economic Limits of Bitcoin and the Blockchain; Mastering Bitcoin ch. 12 (Section 03))*
3. **Gresham vs Thiers, applied** — In ≤ 600 words: state both laws precisely, identify the institutional condition that decides which one applies (legal tender at a fixed exchange rate vs free floating), and apply them to bitcoin and a high-inflation national currency of your choice, anchored on one historical episode from *The Bitcoin Standard* or *The Fiat Standard*. End with one dated, falsifiable prediction about what that country's payment data should show. *(~3 h · Gresham's Law and Thiers' Law concept; The Fiat Standard; The Bitcoin Standard)*
4. **The fee-sniping game** — A miner with hashrate share α sees a tip block carrying unusually high fees F. It can extend the tip (expected value ≈ α × (subsidy + fees left in the mempool)) or re-mine the tip to take F, which requires winning two blocks before the rest of the network finds one (handle ties with γ). Derive the condition under which forking pays, evaluate it with today's numbers and with zero subsidy, and explain how Bitcoin Core's anti-fee-sniping nLockTime weakens the incentive. Then read Carlsten et al.'s undercutting sections and explain how leaving part of F unclaimed recruits other miners to the fork. *(~3 h · Bitcoin Optech: Fee sniping; Carlsten et al., On the Instability of Bitcoin Without the Block Reward; Game theory of mining concept; Mastering Bitcoin ch. 9 (Section 06))*
5. **Stock-to-Flow stress test** — Reproduce the log(market value) ~ log(S2F) regression from PlanB's 2019 stock-to-flow article using only data available at the time, then score its out-of-sample predictions against data through today. Test for spurious regression: check residual stationarity (ADF test) and re-run on first differences. Deliverable: a notebook plus a 300-word verdict on what the model does and doesn't show. *(~4 h · Stock-to-Flow concept; The Bullish Case for Bitcoin)*
