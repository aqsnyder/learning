# Philosophy of AI

## Why This Section Matters

Can machines think? This deceptively simple question, posed by Alan Turing in 1950, launched an entire field. The philosophy of AI examines whether artificial systems can possess genuine intelligence, understanding, or consciousness — or whether they're merely sophisticated mimics. Turing's original paper remains the essential starting point, but the debate has evolved enormously: from Searle's Chinese Room to the symbol grounding problem to modern arguments about large language models. This section brings together the key philosophical arguments about what AI can and cannot achieve.

## Suggested Order

1. **Turing — Computing Machinery and Intelligence (1950)** — The founding document
2. **SEP — The Turing Test** — Context and critique of Turing's proposal
3. **Searle — Minds, Brains, and Programs** — The most powerful objection to strong AI
4. **Harnad — The Symbol Grounding Problem** — Why symbols need bodies
5. **Dreyfus — What Computers Still Can't Do** — The phenomenological critique
6. **SEP — Artificial Intelligence** — Broader philosophical landscape
7. **Bender & Koller — Climbing towards NLU** — The modern version of these debates
8. **Bender et al. — Stochastic Parrots** — Contemporary implications for LLMs
