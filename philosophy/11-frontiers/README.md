# Frontiers

## Why This Section Matters

We are living through an unprecedented convergence: AI systems are becoming powerful enough to force questions that were once purely theoretical. Could an AI be conscious? Should digital minds have rights? Are we living in a simulation? This section collects the cutting-edge work at the intersection of philosophy, AI, and consciousness — from Bostrom's simulation argument to Butlin et al.'s 2023 report on consciousness in AI. These are open questions without consensus answers, and engaging with them is essential for anyone who wants to think seriously about the future of minds and machines.

## Prerequisites

- All previous sections (this section synthesizes themes from across the curriculum)

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:
- Reconstruct Bostrom's simulation argument, compute its key fraction across parameter ranges, and identify the assumptions it rests on (substrate independence, the indifference principle, the reference class).
- Apply the 14 indicator properties of Butlin et al. (2023) to a specific current AI system and state how theory-dependent the verdict is.
- Explain IIT 4.0's core claims, compute system integrated information (φ_s) for toy networks, and state and assess the unfolding argument against causal-structure theories.
- Evaluate Chalmers's case on LLM consciousness and Long, Sebo et al.'s case for taking AI welfare seriously, and state your own credences with reasons.
- Write calibrated, falsifiable predictions about AI-consciousness science and policy, and score them later.

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **The simulation argument, quantified** — Reconstruct Bostrom's trilemma in standard form. Implement f_sim = f_P·f_I·N̄_I / (f_P·f_I·N̄_I + 1) — f_P: fraction of human-level civilizations that reach a posthuman stage; f_I: fraction of those interested in running ancestor simulations; N̄_I: average number of ancestor simulations an interested civilization runs — and plot it over log-ranges of each parameter. How small must f_P·f_I be for f_sim < 0.5 if each interested posthuman civilization runs 10⁶ ancestor simulations? Then identify the weakest premise — substrate independence, the bland indifference principle, or the choice of reference class — and test it against Chalmers's claim in *Reality+* that the simulation hypothesis is not a skeptical hypothesis. 500 words. *(~3 h · Bostrom, "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?"; Chalmers, Reality+)*
2. **Is SPICE a world?** — Chalmers argues that virtual objects are real digital objects. Apply his view to a SPICE simulation of a circuit you know: are the simulated currents real? If so, real in what sense; if not, what distinguishes them from the virtual objects Chalmers defends (interaction with users, persistence, causal powers)? Then turn it around: if we are in a simulation, which of your beliefs about your own lab equipment come out true on Chalmers's view, and which come out false? 600 words. *(~2.5 h · Chalmers, Reality+)*
3. **Indicator audit of a current agent** — Apply Butlin et al.'s 14 indicator properties (RPT-1–2, GWT-1–4, HOT-1–4, AST-1, PP-1, AE-1–2) to one specific 2026 system you use — e.g., a tool-using coding agent with persistent memory. For each indicator: satisfied / partly / not / unknown, the evidence (architecture documentation, observed behavior), and your confidence. Cross-check against the features Chalmers says current LLMs lack (recurrent processing, a global workspace, unified agency, among others). Which indicators are architectural facts you can verify, and which would need interpretability evidence nobody yet has? The rubric assumes computational functionalism as a working hypothesis — say what changes if you drop it and adopt Seth's biological view. *(~4 h · Butlin et al., "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence"; Chalmers, "Could a Large Language Model be Conscious?"; Seth, Being You)*
4. **The unfolding argument, demonstrated** — With PyPhi 2.0, which implements IIT 4.0 (as of October 2026 still a release candidate, 2.0.0rc4: `pip install --pre pyphi`, Python ≥ 3.13; the stable 1.2 release implements the older IIT 3.0), compute φ_s (`.phi`) for a small noisy recurrent network — a 2- or 3-unit loop. Then build a feedforward network, one layer per time step, that reproduces the loop's input–output behavior over T steps, and verify the equivalence by simulating both. Keep it to about six units: a 6-unit analysis already takes minutes per state. IIT says φ_s = 0 for the feedforward version, since it is not strongly connected. State the unfolding argument (from Doerig et al., 2019, which is not among this section's resources; the summary given here is enough for the exercise): if functionally identical systems can differ in IIT-consciousness, no report or behavior can test IIT, so it is either unfalsifiable or false. Then give IIT's best reply — consciousness is intrinsic causal structure, not function (the IIT 4.0 paper; Koch in Section 02) — and judge who has the better of it. 500 words. *(~4 h · Albantakis … Tononi, "IIT 4.0"; Koch, The Feeling of Life Itself (Section 02))*
5. **Prediction ledger** — Write eight dated, probability-weighted predictions that resolve by the end of 2028, each with a precise resolution criterion — e.g., "a pre-registered adversarial collaboration reports a result that proponents of the losing theory accept as disconfirming a core prediction (p = …)"; "a frontier AI developer publishes an AI-welfare policy that restricts some deployment practice (p = …)"; "a national legislature formally debates legal protections for AI systems (p = …)". Include at least two predictions that would embarrass your current views if they came true. Put a reminder in your calendar to score them with a Brier score. *(~2 h · Schwitzgebel, The Weirdness of the World; Long, Sebo et al., "Taking AI Welfare Seriously"; The Alignment Forum; Section 12 on what makes a prediction a test)*

**🏗️ Mini-project — Is This Agent Conscious? A Theory-Relative Assessment:** Write a 3,000-word report on one concrete 2026 AI agent. Sections: (1) the system's architecture as far as public information allows; (2) the Butlin et al. indicator audit (from exercise 3, refined); (3) IIT 4.0's verdict on the actual hardware — and why that verdict is about the chips, not the model; (4) Seth's and Chalmers's verdicts; (5) a credence range with reasons, stated per theory and then aggregated, with Schwitzgebel's case for pervasive uncertainty as the check on overconfidence; (6) a precautionary policy proposal following Long, Sebo et al.'s acknowledge–assess–prepare structure, checked against Birch's precautionary framework: what a developer should do now under this uncertainty, and what evidence would change the recommendation. Deliverable: `projects/philosophy/11-agent-consciousness-report.md`. Success: every verdict is explicitly conditional on a named theory; the report makes at least one falsifiable prediction (an architecture change that should flip specific indicators); no contested theory is presented as settled. *(~12 h)*
