# Eastern Philosophy and Mind

## Why This Section Matters

Western philosophy of mind typically assumes a unified, persistent self — but Buddhist philosophy has questioned this assumption for 2,500 years. The Buddhist doctrine of anattā (non-self) holds that there is no fixed, unchanging self — just a constantly changing stream of physical and mental processes. Classical India answered back: Nyāya philosophers argued that memory, recognition and desire need a self that lasts, and Advaita Vedānta held that the self is consciousness itself. Classical China asked a different question — less what the self is than how the heart-mind (*xin*) should guide action — and produced two models of agency: Mencius's cultivated moral feeling and the *Zhuangzi*'s skilled spontaneity. This is not mere metaphysics: it has deep implications for consciousness studies, personal identity, and AI. If there is no essential self, what are we trying to replicate in AI — and can an agent act well without deliberating? This section bridges Eastern and Western traditions, showing how these insights complement and challenge the Western assumptions underlying the rest of this curriculum.

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:
- Explain the Four Noble Truths, the five aggregates, dependent origination and anattā (non-self) as Rahula presents them.
- Reconstruct the arguments from impermanence and from control in standard form, make the exhaustiveness premise explicit, and test them against the two main Indian theories of the self: Nyāya's enduring self that bears cognition, memory and desire, and Advaita Vedānta's self as pure, self-luminous consciousness.
- Apply the two-truths framework (conventional vs. ultimate) and the chariot argument to persons and to software agents that can be copied, forked or rolled back.
- Explain Nāgārjuna's emptiness (śūnyatā) and the tetralemma, and assess the logical form of the opening argument of the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*.
- Explain the early Chinese picture of the heart-mind (*xin*) and contrast two models of agency — Mencius's cultivation of innate moral "sprouts" and the *Zhuangzi*'s skilled action that no deliberating "pilot in the head" directs — and say what each implies about the self that acts.
- Relate the Buddhist debate over reflexive awareness to Western higher-order theories, and design a sound empirical test of a meditation claim.

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **No-self in standard form, against its Indian rivals** — From the SEP *Buddha* entry and Rahula's chapter on anattā, reconstruct the argument from impermanence and the argument from control as separate standard-form arguments. Make explicit the premise that the five aggregates exhaust what a person is, and the premise that a self would have to be permanent (or wholly under its own control). Then test them against two Indian theories of the self. **Nyāya** (IEP *Nyāya* §2.a; Adamson & Ganeri's chapters on Nyāya): reconstruct its argument from recognition ("that is the very man I saw a week ago": who recognizes, if no one persists?) and its argument that desire, cognition and volition are qualities that need a bearer, and say which Buddhist premise each attacks. **Advaita Vedānta** (IEP *Advaita Vedanta*): its self is pure, self-luminous consciousness, not a thing that has consciousness and not one more item among the aggregates — does the argument from impermanence even touch such a self? 600 words, ending with the premise the Buddhist can least afford to give up. Check: delete the exhaustiveness premise and confirm each Buddhist argument becomes invalid; and state the extra premise the Nyāya argument from recognition needs to be valid (that a causal series of momentary states cannot itself recognize). *(~3.5 h · Rahula, What the Buddha Taught; SEP Buddha; Siderits, Buddhism as Philosophy; Adamson & Ganeri, Classical Indian Philosophy; IEP Nyāya; IEP Advaita Vedanta)*
2. **The chariot and the agent** — The SEP *Buddha* entry uses the chariot as the stock example of a conventionally real whole. Apply the same two-truths analysis to (a) a person, (b) an assembled PCB, and (c) an LLM agent that persists across sessions through memory files and can be forked, rolled back, or run as several copies at once. For each: at the ultimate level, what exists; at the conventional level, what does the name usefully track, and for whom? Where does the analysis give a cleaner answer for the agent than for the person, or vice versa? Compare Parfit's fission verdicts from Section 01. 600 words. *(~2.5 h · SEP Buddha; Siderits, Buddhism as Philosophy; SEP Mind in Indian Buddhist Philosophy)*
3. **First-person data, then a better study** — For 14 days, practice 15 minutes of noting meditation (silently label "seeing", "hearing", "thinking", "planning", "itch" as they arise), following Rahula's chapter on meditation (bhāvanā). Log each session: what you noted, and whether at any point you could find a stable observer distinct from the stream. Map the log onto the five aggregates and write 300 words on what this kind of evidence can and cannot establish, with Thompson's treatment of meditative reports as the foil. Then pick one popular claim ("meditation makes you happier", "meditators show reduced self-referential processing"), audit it in the spirit of Flanagan's critique of neuroscience-of-Buddhism hype and Thompson's case against Buddhist exceptionalism in *Why I Am Not a Buddhist* (what was measured, in whom, against what control?), and design a better study: population, active control, a preregistered primary outcome, blinding where possible, and a sample size. Check: for a two-arm comparison at d = 0.25, α = 0.05, 80% power you should need roughly 250 participants per arm. *(~6 h · Rahula; Thompson, Waking, Dreaming, Being; Flanagan, The Bodhisattva's Brain; Thompson, Why I Am Not a Buddhist; Wright, Why Buddhism Is True)*
4. **Nāgārjuna's tetralemma** — Take the opening verse of chapter 1 of the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (after the dedication): nothing arises from itself, from another, from both, or without a cause. With Garfield's commentary, reconstruct the argument against each of the four modes of arising. Is the overall structure consistent with classical logic (excluded middle, non-contradiction)? Consider the reading on which Nāgārjuna rejects all four options because they share a false presupposition — that things have intrinsic nature (svabhāva). Finally, explain 24:18 (dependent origination = emptiness = the middle way) in your own words and say why emptiness is not nihilism. 600 words. *(~3 h · Nāgārjuna, trans. Garfield, The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way; Siderits, chapter on Madhyamaka)*
5. **Reflexive awareness vs. higher-order theories** — Using Coseru's SEP entry, explain the Buddhist debate over whether every cognition is reflexively self-aware (svasaṃvedana), with the main argument on each side (e.g., the memory argument: we remember not only what we saw but that we saw it). Map the debate onto Section 02's split between higher-order theories (a state is conscious when a separate state represents it) and views on which conscious states are self-presenting. Then apply both to an LLM agent's "reasoning trace" — a token stream about its own earlier tokens: is that higher-order representation, reflexive awareness, or neither? 600 words. *(~3 h · SEP Mind in Indian Buddhist Philosophy; Thompson, Waking, Dreaming, Being; Seth & Bayne, "Theories of Consciousness" (Section 02))*
6. **Cook Ding and the coding agent** — From Wong's SEP entry §§2.4–2.5, set out two models of acting well. Mencius (*Mengzi* 2A6, the child about to fall into a well): the heart-mind begins with innate sprouts of goodness, its job is to lead the rest of the person, and good action comes from cultivating those sprouts. The *Zhuangzi*: the heart-mind is one bodily organ among many, and the best action is skill like Cook Ding's, who after years of practice no longer saw the ox as a whole, and whose body knows the way with no "pilot in the head" directing it. Use Hansen's SEP *Zhuangzi* entry for the text's distrust of any fixed point of view. Apply both models to (a) an experienced engineer who debugs "by feel" and (b) an LLM coding agent that writes fluent code without explicit deliberation. For each: what counts as acting well, what had to be cultivated or trained, and who or what is the agent? 600 words: does the coding agent have anything like the cook's skill, or only its outward form — and is the *Zhuangzi*'s ideal of skilled action with no deliberating self good news or bad news for calling such systems agents? Check: every claim about Mencius or the *Zhuangzi* cites a section of one of the two SEP entries. *(~3 h · Wong, "Mind (Heart-Mind) in Chinese Philosophy" (SEP); Hansen, "Zhuangzi" (SEP))*

**🏗️ Mini-project — The Questions of the Engineer:** Write a 3,000-word dialogue in the manner of the *Questions of King Milinda* between the monk Nāgasena and an engineer who builds persistent AI agents. Who is responsible?"); Nāgārjuna's turn — even the elements are empty; and three agent puzzles: forking (two copies with the same memories), rollback (restoring yesterday's memory file), and deletion (is anything lost that was not merely conventional?).
