# Philosophy of Science and Scientific Reasoning

## Why This Section Matters

Every few sections this curriculum asks a scientific-method question that it gives you no tools to answer. Is Integrated Information Theory pseudoscience, and who "won" the 2025 adversarial test of IIT against global workspace theory (Section 02)? Is Wolfram's ruliad a research programme or a redescription (Section 03)? When a model posts a new benchmark score, has anything been *measured* — intelligence (Section 04), understanding (Section 05), or only the benchmark?

Philosophy of science is the toolkit for these questions:
- what makes a claim testable (Popper);
- how sciences actually change (Kuhn);
- how to tell a research programme that is progressing from one that only patches itself (Lakatos);
- what evidence is in probabilistic terms (Bayesian epistemology) and in error-probability terms (Mayo's severe testing);
- why so many published findings failed to replicate, and whether ML benchmarks are measurements at all.

For an engineer the questions are familiar from test and measurement — could this test have failed, what would failure have looked like, does the metric track the quantity you care about — asked of theories instead of instruments. None of the cases in this section has a settled answer. The goal is to be able to say exactly where, and why, the criteria disagree.

**Recommended position:** take this section right after Section 00. It is numbered 12 only so that existing sections keep their numbers. Its exercises borrow cases from later sections (02, 03, 04); read the specific items they cite, or come back to the exercises after Section 03.

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

After this section you can:
- State Popper's falsifiability criterion, Kuhn's account of normal science and revolutions, and Lakatos's progressive and degenerating research programmes, and explain what each objects to in the view before it.
- Apply four demarcation criteria (Popper, Kuhn, Lakatos, Hansson's definition) to a contested case, show where they disagree, and say what observation would change the verdict — without presenting the case as settled.
- Compute a Bayes factor and an error rate for the same test, explain the problem of the priors, and contrast the Bayesian and error-statistical (Mayo) verdicts on optional stopping.
- Use Ioannidis's positive-predictive-value model to explain how low power and flexible analysis produce unreplicable findings, and say what the Open Science Collaboration's replication rate does and does not show.
- Judge whether a benchmark score measures the construct it is claimed to measure (Raji et al.), and design a re-collection test that separates distribution shift from overfitting (Recht et al.).

## 🛠️ Practice

1. **Demarcation casebook** — Take four cases already in this repo: (a) Integrated Information Theory — Tononi 2004 and IIT 4.0, the 2023 "pseudoscience" letter and the 2025 Cogitate results (Sections 02 and 11); (b) Wolfram's Principle of Computational Equivalence and the ruliad, read with Aaronson's review (Section 03); (c) an ML benchmark claim — the METR time-horizon trend in ML Section 12's Evaluation table, or a frontier model's ARC score read as evidence of "intelligence" (Section 04); (d) a behavioral-economics finding — ego depletion (Econ Section 06's replication note) or the hot-hand "fallacy", whose debunking was itself overturned (Econ Section 03, Practice 5). Build a 4 × 4 matrix: rows are the cases; columns are falsifiability (Popper), puzzle-solving (Kuhn), progressiveness (Lakatos), and Hansson's definition (non-science posing as science, with a doctrinal component). Every cell gets a verdict (meets / fails / indeterminate), the specific evidence, and who would dispute it. Then write two paragraphs per case: the strongest argument that it is good science and the strongest that it is not, each in terms its proponents would accept. Done when no cell says "pseudoscience" without naming its criterion and evidence, at least two cases show criteria that disagree, and each case ends with the observation that would change your verdict. *(~4 h · SEP Science and Pseudo-Science; SEP Karl Popper; Kuhn; Lakatos; Godfrey-Smith)*
2. **Two research programmes, scored** — Treat IIT and global neuronal workspace theory as Lakatosian research programmes. For each, state the hard core and the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses. Then build a dated timeline of significant changes and new predictions since its founding statement (Tononi 2004 through IIT 4.0 in Section 11; Dehaene's *Consciousness and the Brain* for GNWT), tagging each as progressive (it predicted a novel fact that was later corroborated) or degenerating (it absorbed a result after the fact), with a source. Place the 2025 Cogitate results on both timelines. Write a 400-word verdict on which programme is currently more progressive by Lakatos's standard, and name the result in the next round of experiments that would flip it. Then write 200 words from Kuhn's side: can such a verdict be reached except in hindsight, and does Lakatos's criterion tell you when to abandon a programme or only how to describe one? Done when every timeline entry has a dated source and the verdict states its flip condition. *(~3 h · Lakatos; Kuhn; Section 02's Tononi, Dehaene, Lenharo and Cogitate items)*
3. **Peeking, priors and severity, in a notebook** — (a) Optional stopping: draw xᵢ ~ N(0, 1), so H₀: μ = 0 is true and σ = 1 is known. Compute z = x̄·√n after every observation from n = 10 to n = 100, and stop at the first |z| > 1.96. Over 100,000 runs, estimate the false-positive rate. **Check:** about 28% (about 19% if you peek only at n = 10, 20, …, 100), against 5% for a single test at n = 100. (b) Compute the Bayes factor BF₁₀ for H₁: μ ~ N(0, 1) against H₀; under H₀, x̄ ~ N(0, 1/n), and under H₁, x̄ ~ N(0, 1 + 1/n). **Check:** data that just reach z = 1.96 give BF₁₀ ≈ 1.7 at n = 10 but ≈ 0.67 at n = 100, so "p = 0.05" can favor H₀ in a large sample. Confirm that BF₁₀ depends only on the data, not on whether you planned to peek. Then measure how often peeking under H₀ ever reaches BF₁₀ > 3. **Check:** about 11% with peeking, under 1% at a fixed n = 100. (c) Write 500 words. Mayo holds that the peeking design is a less severe test even when the data are identical; many Bayesians hold that the stopping rule is irrelevant to the evidence. Use the SEP *Philosophy of Statistics* entry's discussion of optional stopping and severe testing to adjudicate one real case: an A/B test of a new calibration algorithm on a production line, run until it "looks better". Done when your numbers match the checks within Monte Carlo error and the essay says which property — error control or evidential coherence — you would require of that test, and why. *(~3 h · SEP Philosophy of Statistics; SEP Bayesian Epistemology §1; Mayo, Statistical Inference as Severe Testing, Excursion 4 (Tour III))*
4. **Ioannidis's model meets the replication data** — (a) Implement PPV = (1 − β)R / (R − βR + α) from Ioannidis (2005), where R is the pre-study odds that a tested relationship is true and 1 − β is the power. **Check** (α = 0.05): R = 0.25 with power 0.8 gives 0.80; with power 0.35, 0.64; R = 0.1 with power 0.2 gives 0.29. (b) Model analytic flexibility crudely: a researcher who can choose the best of k independent analyses has an effective α of 1 − (1 − α)^k. **Check:** k = 5 gives α ≈ 0.23, which drops the PPV for R = 0.25 and power 0.35 to ≈ 0.28 if power is held fixed — an assumption to criticize. (c) Invert the Open Science Collaboration's result. If replications have power P against true effects and a false-positive rate α = 0.05 against null ones, a 36% replication rate implies PPV = (0.36 − 0.05)/(P − 0.05). **Check:** P = 0.9 gives ≈ 0.36 and P = 0.6 gives ≈ 0.56. Given that replication effects averaged about half the original size, which P is more realistic? (d) Write 400 words: does the OSC result show that most psychology findings are false? Name at least two other explanations for a failed replication and the data that would distinguish them, and say what the PPV's sensitivity to P does to any headline figure. Done when your code reproduces the checks and every conclusion in the essay is tied to a named parameter. *(~3 h · Ioannidis 2005; Open Science Collaboration 2015; Econ Section 06's replication note)*
5. **A measurement card for a benchmark** — Pick a benchmark claim you actually rely on: a coding-agent leaderboard score, a model's ARC result (Section 04), or a figure from ML Section 12's Evaluation table. Write a one-page "measurement card" in the style of an instrument datasheet. It should cover the construct claimed; the operationalization (task sampling, scoring, aggregation); validity threats, each with evidence (contamination, saturation, distribution shift, Goodhart pressure from Econ Section 11, and the "general capability" overreach Raji et al. describe); and the reproduction evidence. Then run a small re-collection in the style of Recht et al.: write 30 fresh items by the benchmark's own construction rules (for example, 30 new Python functions with unit tests, or 30 ARC-style grids), score one model on them, and compare with its published score using a bootstrap 95% interval (10,000 resamples). Done when the card names at least three validity threats, each with a source, and the comparison reports the interval and says whether a gap looks like distribution shift, overfitting or noise — and how you would tell them apart with more data. *(~5 h · Raji et al. 2021; Recht et al. 2019; Chollet, "On the Measure of Intelligence" (Section 04))*

**🏗️ Mini-project — Referee Report on a Contested Claim:** Choose one contested claim — one of the four casebook cases, or one from your own field, such as a vendor's "AI-calibrated" accuracy claim, a published consciousness metric, or a new benchmark result. Write a 2,500–3,000-word referee report in journal-review format: (1) the claim in its proponents' strongest form; (2) its logical status — what would refute it, and which auxiliary hypotheses it depends on; (3) its record as a research programme (Lakatos); (4) the statistical evidence read two ways — severity (Mayo) and Bayesian (a stated prior, the likelihood ratio, the posterior); (5) its replication status, plus one reproduction you carry out yourself (rerun a published notebook, re-analyze open data, or re-collect a small test set); (6) a recommendation phrased as credences and as "what would change my mind", with the strongest dissent stated in the dissenter's own words. Deliverable: `projects/philosophy/12-referee-report/` (`report.md` plus a `reproduction/` notebook or script). Success: a proponent and a critic of the claim would each accept your statement of their position; every verdict is conditional on a named criterion; the reproduction runs end to end; and the report makes one prediction that could prove it wrong within two years. *(~12 h)*
